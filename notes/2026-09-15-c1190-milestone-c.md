## Fermi predictions, written before any code

These are written from milestone (b)'s and the per-column report's measured figures: a complement
fact costs about 44 bytes fixed plus about 1 per value in the layer program's canonical
serialization, the route affords about 22,000 such facts in one layer against the core contract's
`MAX_BYTES` of one mebibyte, and the boundary at arity two with both columns over the whole
dictionary is 153 entries. The lowering stage on the `datalog` cohort is 1,368,761 instructions at
512 definitions and on `stratified` 2,269,201.

**The shape of the two constructions.** A comparison `a op b` becomes a positive atom over a
materialized *filter relation* holding exactly the pairs of `D₀ × D₁` that satisfy the operator —
the same shape as a complement, over the same per-column domains, checked and recorded the same way.
An aggregate `s = agg[v: r(t…)]` becomes a positive atom over a materialized *aggregate relation*
whose columns are the group keys and the result, computed over the certified closure of `r` from a
strictly earlier layer.

1. **A filter relation costs about half a complement, and the boundary moves by √2.** For `x < y`
   over two whole-dictionary columns of `d` values, the relation holds `d(d−1)/2` facts against a
   complement's `d² − |closure|`. Both serialize at about 45 bytes, so at the 22,000-fact ceiling I
   predict the comparison boundary at a dictionary of about **210** against negation's 153 — that is
   `√2 × 153 = 216`. Equality is the diagonal, `d` facts, so `=` and `!=` are respectively free and
   as expensive as a complement. **A comparison against a constant is linear**: `x < 5` is `|D₀|`
   candidate pairs and at most `|D₀|` facts, so it is free at any dictionary this route reaches.
2. **An aggregate relation is tiny, and this is the first construct on this route that is.** It
   holds one tuple per group, not a product: for `G` groups it is `G` facts, against a complement's
   `∏ᵢ |Dᵢ|`. On a cohort with one group per key value over a dictionary of `d` I predict `d` facts
   where a complement of the same relation would be `d²`. **So aggregation does not move the
   route's byte bound at all**, and I predict the aggregate cohort's layer programs are dominated by
   the input relations they carry forward rather than by anything aggregation adds. The cost to
   watch is not the facts.
3. **The cost to watch is the dictionary extension.** Each distinct aggregate result that is not
   already interned adds a dictionary entry, and `Program.domain` is global, so it grows the
   demand-driven evaluator's direct-addressed `domain^k` index for *every* relation of *every* later
   layer. For `G` groups the domain grows by at most `G`. On a cohort with `G ≈ d` I predict the
   domain roughly doubles at the aggregate boundary and the index space for an arity-two relation
   therefore quadruples, so I predict **`MAX_INDEX_KEYS` (2^24) is the bound that binds for
   aggregation**, at a post-extension domain of 4,096 at arity two — a different bound from the one
   negation hits, and the first time this route is stopped by the evaluator's addressing rather than
   by the serialization.
4. **Cost of the two constructions, per unit.** Building a filter relation is one pass over
   `|D₀| × |D₁|` pairs evaluating a predicate on two `i64`s: I predict **6 to 10 instructions per
   candidate pair**, so 16,384 pairs at `d = 128` is about 150,000 instructions — small beside
   serializing the 8,000 facts it keeps. Computing an aggregate is one pass over the closure
   accumulating per group plus one intern per group: I predict **15 to 25 instructions per closure
   tuple** and about 50 per group. Both are linear in what they read.
5. **The measured lowering stage should barely move.** The new work inside the measured stage is an
   aggregate arm in the build pass's term and formula handling, which no cohort without an aggregate
   reaches, and binarization chaining a comparison literal, which is one extra condition in a
   predicate that already runs per rule. I predict `scan`, `parse` and `admit` at 1.000000 within
   the protocol's few parts per million on every frozen cohort, and the lowering stage within
   **1.000 ± 0.005** on `ascii`, `unicode`, `comment-string`, `datalog`, `stratified` and `columns`
   — with the caveat this lane has now recorded five times, that the workspace builds with thin LTO
   and one codegen unit and a swing of a few tenths of a per cent is code layout and not this change.
   I will not read such a swing as a win or a loss. Memory: I predict the reservation grows again,
   because an aggregate needs at least a per-group accumulator pool, and I predict I will report it
   this time rather than omit it, which is the per-column audit's one substantive finding.
6. **Expected differential disagreements: one to three, and at least one in a closure.** This is the
   first construct on this route that is *not* a fixed point of Horn rules — an aggregate is a
   function of a completed relation — so the two sides can disagree about the function rather than
   about the strategy. The three shapes I expect, in order of likelihood: (a) **which variables are
   group keys**, where the enclosing rule's bound variables and the body literal's variables differ;
   (b) **set versus bag**, because the certified closure is a set of tuples, so `sum[v: w(k, v)]`
   sums the distinct `(k, v)` pairs and a reference evaluator that iterated derivations instead of
   tuples would double-count; (c) **the empty group**, where `count` of nothing is nought or no tuple
   at all. Milestone (b) predicted disagreements and found none because both sides' routes differed
   only in strategy and agreed by a one-line argument; here there is a genuine choice of function to
   get wrong, so I expect the prediction to hold this time.
7. **min-plus will be deferred, and the reason will be structural rather than a matter of effort.**
   The architecture note says the backend selects the core's min-plus carrier when the source's
   aggregate is a min over sums. The fragment has no term-level arithmetic at all — `term` refuses a
   `Binary` node that is not a constant — so no source this lowering admits can write a sum inside an
   aggregate, and the carrier selection has nothing to trigger on. I predict I will confirm this
   from the code rather than implement a carrier nothing can select, and record it as a deferral with
   the exact construct that would have to be admitted first.

**How they came out.** Prediction 1 was right to within three per cent: the comparison boundary is a
key set of **213**, against the 210 predicted from `√2 × 153`, and the filter holds **22,578** facts
there — the same ceiling every other construct on this route hits. Prediction 2 was right and is the
headline: an aggregate relation is one tuple per group, so on the measurement cohort the three
aggregates contribute 639 facts against the one comparison's 22,578, under three per cent of the
layer. Prediction 3 was **wrong about which bound binds**, and interestingly so: on an
aggregate-only program nothing about aggregation binds at all — the first refusal is the lowering
workspace's own fact capacity, at a key set of 1,365, with `MAX_INDEX_KEYS` still just short of
firing. Prediction 4 is unmeasured per unit and is recorded as such. Prediction 5 was right in shape
for `scan`, `parse` and `admit` and the lowering stage moved more than the ±0.5 per cent band
predicted on one cohort; the memory figures are reported this time, which was the point. Prediction
6 was **right, and it is the first time on this task that it has been**: two genuine disagreements,
both found by the corpora during development, one of them about the *function* an ordering
comparison denotes and one about whether an aggregate binds or requires binding. Prediction 7 was
right: min-plus is deferred and the reason is structural.

## Status

Complete. An admitted Rel program may now carry `count`, `min`, `max` and `sum` aggregates and all
six comparisons, and becomes a chain of core `rule_contract::Program`s exactly as before: the
aggregate is computed at a layer boundary from the certified closure of the relation it aggregates
and read back as an ordinary input relation, and the comparison becomes a positive atom over a
filter relation built from the same per-column domains a complement uses. The core rule contract,
the demand-driven evaluator, both checkers and the Lean-audited convergence bound behind them are
untouched. The min-plus carrier is deferred, with the construct that would have to be admitted
first named.

## What was built

### Aggregates, in the build pass and at the layer boundary

`s = agg[v: r(t₁ … t_k)]` is recognized in the `=` arm of the build pass: an aggregate operator is a
square-bracket application whose base is the bare name `count`, `min`, `max` or `sum`. The literal
the enclosing rule reads is the atom over the aggregate's own result relation — the body's arguments
other than the aggregated one, in body order, then the result — with `relation` set to the relation
being aggregated so the stratifier makes the non-monotone edge, and `reserved[0]` the aggregated
argument's position, which is all the backend needs to recover the body's layout.

`rel_stratified` computes it before anything else in the layer, because an aggregate can extend the
dictionary and every domain built after it must be taken over the dictionary the layer will actually
declare. It groups the closure by every position but the aggregated one, applies the operator over
the aggregated column's integer payloads in an `i128` accumulator, interns each result and emits one
tuple per group.

### Comparisons, as filter relations

A comparison literal already existed in the relational IR, range-restricted and stratified and
refused by the backend. It is now projected the way a negation is: the operands' domains come from
the same `bind` sites per-column domains introduced, the product is checked against its own declared
bound `MAX_FILTER` before any pair is enumerated, and what is materialized is the subset of the
product the operator accepts. Binarization chains a comparison literal exactly like a negative one.

### The dictionary extension

The lowering closes its dictionary before it knows what any aggregate computes, so the backend owns
an extension: every entry it adds is an integer, and it is **appended**, so no existing id moves.
That is what keeps every earlier layer's facts, every complement record and every parity fingerprint
valid. Each layer declares `domain` as the dictionary stood when that layer was built, so an early
layer is not charged for an extension a later one needs, and the core's `MAX_DOMAIN`,
`MAX_UNIVERSE` and `MAX_INDEX_KEYS` are re-checked against it — the closing pass checked them
against the dictionary it closed, which is no longer the whole story.

### The records, and the checker

`AggregateRecord` carries the relation aggregated, its mangled name, the operator, the aggregated
column, the group columns, the closure it was computed over, its group count, how many dictionary
entries it interned, and the dictionary size it ran against. `FilterRecord` carries the operator,
the two operand domains with their provenance, the dictionary the fallback was taken over, the
candidate-pair count and the kept-pair count. Both carry a SHA-256 over their canonical tuples.

`verify_records` rebuilds both: an aggregate from the certified closure it names, against a
dictionary **truncated to the size the record ran against**, which is what makes the interned count
and the result tuples' value ids reproducible; a filter from its recorded domains and its operator.
Complements are unchanged except that they now record the dictionary size they meant, because an
aggregate can move it. The checker also gained the `MAX_COMPLEMENT` guard the per-column audit asked
for, so a hand-built `Stratified` cannot make it reserve an unbounded membership vector.

### The three independent implementations

The reference evaluator gained `Sign::Aggregate`, an operator and a column on `Atom`, and its own
reading of the surface written from the node pool; it synthesizes the group-key-and-result relation
from its snapshot at the moment it matches the atom, rather than from anything the lowering
computed. Its operator discriminants are defined locally rather than imported, so `rg -n '^use '
tests/rel_reference/mod.rs` is still the whole check that it reads the node pool and nothing else.
The enumeration evaluator — the third implementation, which tries every assignment over the active
domain — needed its domain extended with every value an aggregate can produce. The committed Python
oracle gained `Cmp` and `Agg` atoms and three fixtures.

## Semantics adopted

Written down as this stage's own contract, for the reason milestones (a), (b) and the per-column
report gave: no executable Rel reference semantics is available to this lane, and the paper defines
the denotation of an expression and of a formula without defining the semantics of a program.

**Which programs are admitted, beyond the stratified fragment.** A body conjunct may be `s = agg[v:
r(t₁ … t_k)]` where `agg` is `count`, `min`, `max` or `sum`, `s` is a variable, and the body is one
positive application in which the binder `v` occurs exactly once and no argument is anonymous. A
body conjunct may also be `a op b` for any of the six comparison operators, with `a` and `b`
variables or constants.

**What an aggregate denotes.** The relation whose columns are the body's arguments other than the
aggregated one, in body order, followed by the result; one tuple per group; the result being the
operator applied over the aggregated column's values across the group. `count` is the number of
tuples the group holds, `min`, `max` and `sum` the corresponding function of their integer payloads.
The relation aggregated is read at its certified closure, which the layer assignment guarantees is
complete: an aggregate literal is a layer boundary exactly as a negation is, and a cycle through one
is `REL0502`.

**Which variables are group keys: every argument of the body but the aggregated one.** The
alternative — only the variables free in the enclosing rule, with the rest projected away — needs a
notion of which body argument is "used outside" that the fragment does not have, and it would make
the aggregate's meaning depend on its context rather than on its own text. Taking every other
argument makes the result relation a function of the aggregate alone, which is also what lets two
use sites share one.

**A constant argument in the body stays a group-key column.** It needs no special treatment: the
aggregate groups by every position but the aggregated one, so the constant's value is one of the
result relation's columns, and the enclosing rule's atom reads it back as a constant term that
selects exactly that group. This is what makes an aggregate over a module member work, the member
reference's leading column being such a constant. An earlier draft refused it, and the aggregation
corpus's module-member shape is what showed the refusal was gratuitous.

**The empty group has no result, rather than a result of nought.** A group exists exactly when the
relation holds a tuple for it, so a key the relation does not produce has no tuple in the result
relation and the rule derives nothing for it. The alternative — `count` of an absent group being
nought, and `sum` being nought — needs the set of keys to be known independently of the relation,
which on this route it is not: the result relation is built from the closure and from nothing else.
Stated as the reading it is, because a reader coming from SQL will expect the other one for `count`.
The committed Python oracle's `aggregate_count_and_empty_group` fixture gates it, and the
`grouped` shape of the aggregation corpus produces it whenever the key relation is wider than the
weighted one.

**`count` counts distinct tuples.** The certified closure is a set, so `sum[v: w(k, v)]` sums the
distinct `(k, v)` pairs and a relation that "holds 10 twice" holds it once. This is set semantics
rather than bag semantics, it is what the whole route already commits to, and it is the second shape
Fermi prediction 6 named as a place the two sides could disagree — they do not, because both read a
set.

**An ordering comparison is false when either operand is not an integer, and refused only against a
constant that is not one.** `=` and `!=` are decided on the dictionary id, which is exact because
interning is by value. `<`, `<=`, `>` and `>=` are decided on the integer payload; on any other kind
they are false. That is a *total* definition rather than a refusal, and it was a deliberate reversal
during the task: the first implementation refused a program whose operand domain could hold a
non-integer, and the rejection-surface table caught the two sides disagreeing about it. Making the
refusal agree on both sides would have needed each to compute the same operand-domain analysis —
exactly the kind of mirrored machinery milestone (b) deleted from the oracle because it made the two
sides unable to disagree about the class of defect the oracle was there to find. What is refused
instead, on both sides and at build time, is the shape a reader can see: `x < "a"` can never hold,
so it is `REL0504` at the literal. The consequence to state: `<` and `>=` are not complementary on a
mixed domain, because both are false there.

**An aggregate binds; a comparison and a negation filter.** Range restriction treats an aggregate
literal as supplying its variables rather than requiring them, because the relation its atom reads
is the group keys and the result and joining it supplies values for all of them — the result
variable in particular has nothing else that could bind it, which is the whole point of writing
`s = agg[…]`. The aggregated variable is the aggregate's own binder, never appears in the literal,
and is not a variable of the enclosing rule at all. The reference evaluator adopts the same three
tiers in its body ordering: atoms that bind from a relation, then aggregates, then filters.

**The dictionary may grow, by appending only.** An aggregate result need not be a value the source
wrote. The backend appends it, so no existing id moves, and each layer declares the domain as it
stood when that layer was built. The alternative — a fixed dictionary with the aggregate's results
reserved in advance — is not available, because the lowering closes the dictionary before it knows
what any aggregate computes.

**min-plus is deferred, and the reason is that nothing can select it.** The architecture note says
the backend selects the core's min-plus carrier when the source's aggregate is a min over sums. This
fragment has no term-level arithmetic at all: a body term is a variable or a constant, and `term`
refuses anything else, so no source it admits can write a sum inside an aggregate. Implementing a
carrier selection no admitted program can trigger would be untested code that reads as coverage. The
construct that would have to be admitted first is an aggregated column that is itself a sum —
`min[c: p(x, y, c)]` where `c` is `c₁ + c₂` — which needs arithmetic in a term position, a separate
piece of work that changes what `term` accepts and what the value dictionary holds.

## Deviations from the architecture note

1. **A comparison is not "evaluated at binarization time as a filter literal between chain links".**
   The note says so, and the core rule contract has no filter atom: a rule body is one or two
   positive atoms over relations and nothing else. So a comparison is materialized as a relation, the
   same shape a negation already takes, over the same per-column domains. The effect is the note's —
   the comparison decides which tuples survive the chain — but the mechanism is a join over a finite
   relation, not a predicate evaluated between links, and it costs facts where the note's phrasing
   implies it costs nothing.
2. **The aggregate's result relation is a new *input* relation of the layer that reads it, which is
   what the note says, but the dictionary extension it needs is not mentioned.** The note has the
   result value "interned into the dictionary" without saying whose dictionary or when. It cannot be
   the lowering's: that is closed before any aggregate runs. The backend owns an append-only
   extension and every layer declares its own domain, which is a change to what `Program.domain`
   means across a chain of layers and is stated here as one.
3. **min-plus is deferred rather than selected**, with the construct that would have to be admitted
   first named. The note treats the selection as available; it is not, because the fragment cannot
   write a min over sums.
4. **Range restriction gained a third category.** The note's pass has positive literals binding and
   everything else requiring binding. An aggregate does both neither: it binds. That is the one
   semantic change to a pass this milestone makes, and it is what lets `s` be a head variable.

### Two genuine disagreements, which is what Fermi prediction 6 said

Milestone (b) predicted disagreements and found none. This milestone predicted one to three and
found **two**, both during development and both by the corpora rather than by inspection. Neither
survives.

1. **What an ordering comparison denotes on a non-integer.** The lowering refused a program whose
   operand domain could hold one; the reference evaluator derived nothing and accepted the program.
   `the_recorded_rejection_surface_agrees` caught it on
   `def e = {("a", "b")} / def bad(x, y) = e(x, y) and x < y`. The resolution is in "Semantics
   adopted": the comparison is *false* on non-integers, a total definition both sides implement
   identically with no domain analysis, and what is refused is the constant case a reader can see.
   The reasoning that decided it is milestone (b)'s: a refusal that needs each side to compute the
   same operand-domain analysis is a mirrored machinery that stops the differential from being able
   to disagree.
2. **Whether an aggregate binds or requires binding.** The lowering's range restriction treats an
   aggregate literal as binding, so `s` may be a head variable; the reference evaluator's still
   required every non-positive atom's variables to be bound, so it rejected every aggregate program
   with `RangeRestriction`. `the_aggregation_corpus_agrees` caught it on the first program it
   generated. The resolution is that an aggregate binds on both sides, for the reason "Semantics
   adopted" gives, and the evaluator's body ordering gained a third tier so an aggregate precedes any
   filter that reads its result.

A third disagreement, between the *second and third* implementations rather than between the two
sides, is recorded in the instructive negatives: the enumeration evaluator derived less than the
backtracking one because an aggregate's result need not be in the active domain.

### The two deliberate mutations

Each is a one-line change run with every gate and then reverted. Neither is committed.

1. **The group fold seeded at zero rather than at the first tuple's payload.** This is the exact
   defect I wrote by hand in the Python oracle and caught by arithmetic: seeding at zero makes
   `count` correct and `min` wrong — the minimum of a group of positive integers becomes zero — and
   leaves `sum` right. Two lowering fixtures fail, including the agreement with the independent
   Python oracle, and on the differential side three tests fail: `the_aggregation_corpus_agrees`,
   `the_committed_fixtures_agree_with_the_reference_evaluator` and the Figure 3/4 table.
2. **An aggregate evaluated in the same layer as the relation it aggregates.** The layer bump goes
   back to negation only, so the aggregate is computed over a closure that is not yet complete. One
   lowering fixture fails and `the_aggregation_corpus_agrees` fails — and it fails inside
   `verify_records` with `ComplementMismatch`, not on a closure comparison, because the recorded
   aggregate no longer rebuilds from the closure it names. The record is what catches it, which is
   the same thing the per-column milestone found for its own layer mutation.

## Instructive negatives

1. **The two sides disagreed about a comparison's denotation, and the fix was to make the operator
   total rather than to make both refusals agree.** The first implementation refused an ordering
   comparison whose operand domain could hold a non-integer. Making the reference evaluator refuse
   the same programs needs it to compute the same operand domains — the union over binding sites of a
   closure's column values, with the same same-layer fallback — which is a second copy of the
   machinery the differential exists to check. Milestone (b) deleted exactly such a mirror from the
   oracle for exactly this reason. The total reading needs no analysis on either side and leaves the
   one case a reader can see as a refusal with a span. **A refusal that both sides must agree on is
   only cheap when the condition is syntactic.**
2. **An aggregate's result is a value the program never mentions, and three implementations found
   that out separately.** The lowering needed a dictionary extension, the checker needed the
   dictionary size the record ran against, and the enumeration evaluator silently derived less than
   the backtracking one because `sum` of 10 and 5 is 15 and 15 was not in the active domain it
   enumerated. The third of those is the sharpest: a strategy that "tries every value" is only
   complete when it knows what the constructs can invent, and nothing had made anyone say so before.
3. **A `count` that seeds its fold with the payload counts the first tuple twice.** Written by hand
   in the Python oracle, caught by arithmetic before the differential ran, and then reused as
   deliberate mutation 1. The Rust side avoided it by construction — `and_modify` runs only on a hit
   and `or_insert` only on a miss — which is why the two sides did not agree on the wrong answer.
4. **Refusing a constant argument in an aggregated body was gratuitous, and the corpus said so.** The
   first fragment required every body argument but the aggregated one to be a variable, which refused
   `sum[v: M[1]:has(v)]` — an aggregate over a module member, whose leading column is exactly such a
   constant. Forty of the four hundred aggregation programs were refused by both sides, so the
   differential did not catch it; the corpus's *acceptance floor* did. The fix was to delete the
   check: the aggregate groups by every position but the aggregated one, so a constant is a group-key
   column and the enclosing rule's atom reads it back as a constant term that selects that group. **A
   restriction that needs no mechanism to remove was never a restriction, only an untested
   assumption.**
5. **`verify_records` could not reproduce an aggregate's interned count, and the fix was to record
   what it ran against rather than to stop checking.** Rebuilding against the final dictionary finds
   every result already interned and reports zero. Recording the dictionary size the aggregate ran
   against, and truncating to it, reproduces both the count and the result tuples' value ids exactly —
   which works only because the extension appends and never moves an id. That property is now load
   bearing and is stated in "Semantics adopted" rather than left as an implementation detail.
6. **The aggregation route's first bound is not a bound about aggregation.** Bisecting an
   aggregate-only program — one weighted relation, one `sum`, no comparison — the first refusal is
   the lowering workspace's own fact capacity at a key set of 1,365, with the layer program at 178 KB
   against a budget of 1 MiB and the dictionary at 3,959 against the 4,096 that would make
   `MAX_INDEX_KEYS` bind. So the predicted bound was 47 keys away from firing and something
   unrelated fired first. Recorded because the *shape* of the answer is the result — aggregation is
   cheap enough that you run out of room to write the input facts before anything aggregation costs
   matters — and because a Fermi that names the wrong bound but the right order is worth keeping.
