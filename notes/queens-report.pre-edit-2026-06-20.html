1What the game actually is

flip this section

Picture a chessboard. A queen attacks every square in her row, her column, and both diagonals — long lines that shoot across the whole board.

Two players take turns. On your turn you place one queen on any empty square that is not attacked by any queen already on the board. So every queen you add is safe from all the others, and it also "poisons" a lot of squares for the future. The board steadily fills up with attacked squares.

Eventually someone faces a board where every empty square is under attack — they cannot move, and they lose. That is the whole game.

The question we answer is not "what is a good move." It is the absolute question: with both players playing perfectly, does the player who goes first win, or the player who goes second? For a 16×16 board the answer is: the second player wins. Proving that took exploring billions of possible games.

The one picture to keep

The board is divided into "available" squares (empty and safe — your legal moves) and everything else. Each move you make can only shrink the available set, never grow it. The game is a slow collapse, and whoever makes the last legal placement wins.

This is the adversarial game studied by Noon & Van Brummelen (2006), not the n-queens placement puzzle. Two players alternately place mutually-non-attacking queens on an n×n board; under normal play the player who cannot move loses. A queen on square s blocks its entire row, column, and both diagonals.

The position is captured completely by a single blocked mask = (occupied ∪ attacked). Because a queen is colourless and placing one on s always adds the same fixed attack(s), any two move-orders reaching the same blocked mask are identical for all future play. The set of legal moves is the available set:

\( \text{available} = \text{board} \;\wedge\; \neg\,\text{blocked} \)

and |available| (its popcount) is monotone non-increasing down the search tree — a fact the solver exploits heavily (see §9–10). The board is a 256-bit mask: Bits([u64; 4]), bit r·n + c for square (r,c), so up to 16×16 fits in four words. Attack masks are precomputed once per square (attack[s], self-blocking).

// geom.rs — a square attacks its row, col, and both diagonals (and itself)
if rr == r || cc == c
   || (rr as i32 - cc as i32) == (r as i32 - c as i32)   // ╲ diagonal
   || (rr + cc) == (r + c)                                // ╱ diagonal
{ mask.set(rr * n + cc); }

Move order is static and forcing: squares are sorted by descending attack-degree (order), so the most board-closing moves are tried first and α-β cutoffs fire early. For this blocking game that static order is near-optimal — dynamic move ordering was measured as a net loss.

Where the game came from

flip this section

The familiar n-queens puzzle asks for one completed arrangement. This game asks a different question: if two people build such an arrangement one queen at a time, who can force the last move? A restricted strategy-game version appeared in print by 1989. Hassan Noon studied the modern game in a 2002 undergraduate thesis, then published it with Glen Van Brummelen in The College Mathematics Journal in 2006. Their work settled the odd boards and the small even boards through 10.

Thomas Jenrich encountered the question through a later open-problem list and independently attacked it with a tiny Pascal backtracking program. During publication he learned of Noon's earlier work. His 2014 paper supplied simple strategies for n=4 and every odd n, and computational verdicts for the even boards through n=16. That 16×16 run—almost 23 hours on a 1 GHz Pentium III—remained the independent result this project uses as its full-scale correctness check.

The problem sits at the intersection of several fields: recreational chessboard mathematics; combinatorial game theory, which studies perfect-information games without chance; graph games, because each available square is a vertex in Node Kayles; and exact exponential algorithms, where symmetry, memoization, proof cutoffs, and external-memory duplicate detection decide what can actually be computed.

The bibliographic trail has two branches. G. Schrage's 1989 “eight queens problem as a strategy game” mentions a restricted form. Hassan A. Noon's 2002 B.A. thesis, Surreal Numbers and the N-Queens Game, and Noon & Van Brummelen's 2006 College Mathematics Journal article are the original sources for the present rules and results through odd n / n≤10. Jenrich's 2014 paper and QPGAME3 source state that he found the earlier work only during submission; his starting prompt came from Martin Erickson's open-problem list.

Mathematically, the available-square conflict graph turns the game into Node Kayles, connecting it to impartial combinatorial game theory and Sprague–Grundy values; outcome determination for general Node Kayles is PSPACE-complete. Computationally, this instance family belongs to exact game solving / exponential search: exploit board automorphisms, merge transpositions, preserve first-losing-child cutoffs, and control the memory hierarchy. The OEIS A344227 record gives full nimbers through n=13, not just win/loss outcomes.

What Jenrich actually computed—and how it would parallelize today

Jenrich did not remember every board he had seen. He searched the same future again whenever a different move order reached it, but kept the program tiny and used symmetry plus a hand-built opening book for the second player's first two replies. The 16×16 proof made 71,461,975,237 recursive calls. Its 28 independent opening cases could be handed to separate modern cores with almost no communication. Even on the original machine, perfect root scheduling would reduce the 22h55m total to roughly the longest opening case—about 1h26m. A modern port would be much faster again, but quoting an exact time without compiling and benchmarking that code would be fiction.

QPGAME3 is general backtracking with bit-pattern row/column/diagonal legality, full D₄ restriction only at the first move, conditional half-turn symmetry, move-order/“forbidden” pruning, and a hand-constructed book forcing player 2's first and second replies at n=16. It uses no heap, pointer structure, or TT; memory is a few KiB. The published run totals 71.462 B calls across 28 symmetry-distinct first-move cases in 22h55m30s. Those cases are embarrassingly parallel. Their largest case is 4.471 B calls, so the hardware-independent ideal root-parallel critical path is 4.471/71.462 = 6.26% of serial, or ~1h26m at the original per-call rate (16.0×, not 28×, because cases are imbalanced). On a many-core modern CPU it should scale cleanly at this coarse boundary because it has no shared TT or memory-bandwidth pressure. Further splitting inside a case is less clean: speculative siblings can destroy the first-losing-child cutoff. It would remain algorithmically expensive—71 B visits versus 1.926 B current nodes—so a measured modern port is needed before making a wall-time claim.

2Why it is hard

flip this section

You might think a computer could just try every game. The trouble is the sheer number of distinct ways a game can unfold. Each early move spawns dozens of follow-ups, each of those spawns dozens more, and the tree of possibilities explodes.

There is a saving grace: many different move-orders end up at the exact same board. If you remember boards you have already solved, you avoid redoing them. But on a 16×16 board, the number of genuinely different boards you must remember is in the billions — far more than fits comfortably in a computer's memory. So the real fight is not "thinking faster," it is "remembering billions of boards without running out of room."

3Impartial games and who wins

flip this section

This game has a special, very clean property: it does not matter who placed which queen. A queen is just a queen. Both players have exactly the same moves available from any given board. Games like this (think Nim, or this game) are called impartial, and they are completely understood by mathematicians.

For an impartial game where the last player to move wins, every board is one of two types:

  • A losing board for whoever has to move (a "P-position" — Previous player wins). Whatever you do, you hand your opponent a winning board.
  • A winning board (an "N-position" — Next player wins): at least one of your moves hands the opponent a losing board.

So a board is winning exactly when you can move to a losing board. That single rule, applied recursively all the way down, decides everything. The empty 16×16 board turns out to be a losing board for the first player — which is the same as saying the second player wins.

A free shortcut for odd boards

On any odd-sized board (5×5, 7×7, …) the first player wins instantly with no computer search at all: play the exact center, then simply mirror every move your opponent makes through the center. Your mirror move is always legal, so you always have a reply, so your opponent runs out first. Only even boards (like 16×16) require the heavy search.

The game is impartial (moves depend only on the position, not the player) and normal-play (last to move wins). The recursive win/loss relation is the negamax characterization:

\( \text{wins}(P) \;=\; \exists\, \text{move } m:\; \neg\,\text{wins}(P \text{ after } m) \)

A position with no moves is a loss for the player to move. Everything else folds from there. The empty 16×16 board evaluates to a loss for the mover ⇒ second player wins, matching Jenrich (arXiv:1312.5135) and OEIS A344227 (our verdicts agree through n=13; the win/loss verdict holds through 16).

The odd-n O(1) theorem

Odd boards are decided without search by a pairing strategy: the first player takes the center, then answers each reply with its 180° rotation. The mirror move is always legal because a square attacks its own 180° image only on a center line, so off-center the pairing survives to the end. The solver short-circuits this:

fn first_player_wins(&self, q) -> bool {
    if q.is_odd() { return true; }   // centre + 180° mirror, no search
    /* … the real search for even n … */
}
The formal version — P-positions, N-positions, and why the recursion is sound

For an impartial normal-play game, define the set of P-positions (previous-player-wins, i.e. the mover loses) as the unique set \(\mathcal P\) satisfying: (i) every terminal position is in \(\mathcal P\); (ii) from every position in \(\mathcal P\), every move leads outside \(\mathcal P\); (iii) from every position not in \(\mathcal P\), some move leads into \(\mathcal P\). Existence and uniqueness follow by induction on the (finite, acyclic-by-monotone-popcount) game DAG.

The win/loss bit is exactly \( \mathbb 1[P \notin \mathcal P] \). Because the available popcount strictly decreases on every move, the game graph is a DAG with bounded depth \(\le n^2\), so the recursion terminates and memoization is well-defined. Soundness of transposition merging follows from the impartial, history-free structure: the value of a node depends only on its blocked mask, never on the path taken to reach it — so there is no graph-history-interaction (GHI) hazard, and identical masks may share a stored value.

4Symmetry — the first big way to visit fewer positions

flip this section

A square board looks the same after you rotate it a quarter turn, or flip it like a pancake. There are eight such views of any board (4 rotations × the mirror image of each). All eight are, for the purposes of this game, the same position — if you can win from one, you can win from all of them.

So before the computer stores a board, it asks: "of my eight views of this board, which one looks the 'smallest' by a fixed rule?" It always stores that one. Now eight boards that are rotations/reflections of each other collapse into a single remembered entry — up to 8× less to remember and re-solve.

The dihedral group D₄ — the symmetry group of the square — has 8 elements: identity, three rotations (90/180/270°), and four reflections (two axis flips, two diagonal flips). It acts on board squares as a permutation. Two boards in the same D₄ orbit are the same game, so we store only the canonical representative: the lexicographically-smallest image under all 8 transforms.

fn canon(&self, mask) -> Bits {            // geom.rs
    let mut best = mask;
    for t in 1..8 {                        // the 7 non-identity symmetries
        let img = permute(mask, &self.sym[t]);
        if img < best { best = img; }       // lexicographic min over the orbit
    }
    best
}
fn pos_key(&self, blocked) -> Bits {
    self.canon(self.board.and_not(blocked)) // canonicalise AVAILABLE, not blocked
}

One choice here matters more than it looks: the solver canonicalises the available mask, not the blocked mask. Both merge the same equivalence classes (available is a pure function of blocked), but available has far fewer set bits deep in the tree, so canon shuffles proportionally fewer bits per call. Switching to it cut n=14 from 79.6 s to 33.0 s — a 2.4× speedup with byte-identical node counts, purely from a cheaper key.

3Blue1Brown-style intuition · group actions & orbits

Think of the 8 symmetries as a little machine that "shuffles" a board into one of (up to) 8 look-alikes. Run any board through all 8 settings and you get its orbit — its family of identical-twins. Picking the canonical representative is like alphabetizing the family and always filing under the first name: no matter which twin you start from, you file under the same name, so the whole family lands in one folder. That is why it is well-defined and why it merges the family into one stored entry.

The formal version — D₄ orbits and Burnside counting

\(D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = 1,\; srs = r^{-1}\rangle\), order 8. It acts on the set of board states; the orbit of a state \(x\) is \(\mathcal O(x) = \{g\cdot x : g \in D_4\}\), and by orbit–stabilizer \(|\mathcal O(x)| = |D_4| / |\mathrm{Stab}(x)| \in \{1,2,4,8\}\) (most positions have trivial stabilizer ⇒ orbit size 8). The number of distinct positions up to symmetry is the orbit count, given by Burnside's lemma:

\( \displaystyle \#\text{orbits} \;=\; \frac{1}{|D_4|}\sum_{g \in D_4} |\mathrm{Fix}(g)| \)

where \(\mathrm{Fix}(g)\) is the set of positions invariant under \(g\). Most positions are fixed only by the identity, so the average is close to \(\tfrac18\) of the raw count — hence the ~8× reduction. The canonical-representative trick computes the same quotient constructively: \(\mathrm{key}(x) = \min_{g}\, g\cdot x\) under the lexicographic order on the bitset, which is constant on orbits by construction.

Roots: how symmetry shrinks the very first move

The same idea pays off before the search even begins. A naive program would treat all 256 squares of a 16×16 board as 256 different opening moves. But rotate or flip the board and it's the same game — so the eight mirror-images of any opening are really one opening. Fold the board by its symmetries and only a small wedge of genuinely-different first moves is left. Those are the roots the solver actually starts from.

D₄ acts on the opening square too. Canonicalising a first move to its orbit representative (the lexicographically-minimal of its 8 images) collapses ~8 openings into one, so the solver only launches a search from each distinct orbit. The number of roots is the number of D₄ orbits of board squares — about ⌈n²/8⌉, lifted slightly because squares on a symmetry axis (the diagonal or the mid-lines) have smaller orbits (size 4, or 1 at the centre), not the full 8.

5The available set as a graph — merging far more

flip this section

Symmetry merges boards that are rotations of each other. But there is a deeper kind of sameness. Forget the board entirely and look only at the structure of the legal moves: which available squares "conflict" with which other available squares (because one attacks the other).

Draw a dot for each available square, and a line between two dots whenever those squares attack each other. You get a network (a graph). The payoff: two boards that look nothing alike — different attacked regions, different positions, even different rotations — can produce the same network shape. When the networks match, the games are identical, with the same winner every time.

This merges far more positions than symmetry alone — roughly 3.4× more, on top of the 8× from symmetry. The catch is that checking whether two networks have the same shape is expensive. iso-flat split the difference: graph handling on small networks and plain board symmetry on large ones. Today's iso-dense goes further: it computes every small-network answer directly with W8–W12, so it no longer needs to name or remember those graphs at all.

The available squares form the available-graph: one vertex per available square, an edge between two vertices whenever the squares mutually attack. Since the game is impartial Node Kayles on exactly this graph, positions with isomorphic available-graphs have identical game values — a strictly coarser (more merging) equivalence than D₄. Measured safe merge: 2.63 / 3.17 / 3.42 / 3.40× at n=8/10/12/14, plateauing at ~3.4× ⇒ n=16's ~7.2B D₄-distinct collapses to ~2.1B graph-distinct.

Graph canonicalization is the cost. A full Weisfeiler–Leman (color-refinement) canonical key costs ~100× the D₄ key (~4200 cycles vs ~62), making a pure graph-keyed search a live loss despite 3.4× fewer nodes. The resolution is a selective key in disjoint tagged namespaces:

fn node_key(&self, q, orient) -> Bits {        // iso_flat.rs
    let pc = avail.popcount();
    if pc <= self.iso_max_avail {              // small graph: exact iso, cheap
        let h = if pc <= 7 { iso_key_tiny_table(avail) }  // complete ≤7 lookup
                else        { iso_key_fast(avail) };       // WL canon
        graph_bits(h)                          // tagged graph namespace
    } else {
        d4_bits(lex_min8(orient))              // large graph: cheap D4, disjoint tag
    }
}

For ≤7-vertex graphs the value is resolved by a complete tiny table keyed by the raw labelled edge code (a 28-bit upper-triangular bitstring) — no canonicalization at all, just one direct byte load, because the Node-Kayles value is relabelling-invariant. The default threshold is 7 (the top of the no-WL regime). The two namespaces (graph_bits / d4_bits) never collide even on a full 16×16 board where no spare board bit exists.

Lineage note: the selective-key code above explains iso-flat and why graph equivalence was a crucial discovery. In today's iso-dense K=12 hot path it is bypassed: pc≤12 is evaluated by WK with no canonical key or TT entry, while pc≥13 uses the carried D₄ representative. The champion inherits the graph-game model, not the live WL canonicalizer.

3Blue1Brown-style intuition · graph isomorphism & color refinement

Two graphs are "the same shape" if you can rename the dots of one to turn it into the other. Checking that directly is brutal — you would try every renaming. Color refinement sneaks up on it: paint every dot by how many neighbours it has. Then re-paint every dot by the multiset of its neighbours' colors. Repeat. Dots that are structurally different drift to different colors; dots that play the same role keep matching colors. After a few rounds the coloring stabilizes, and the sorted list of final colors is a fingerprint of the shape — cheap to compare, and it almost always tells isomorphic graphs apart. That is Weisfeiler–Leman, and it is what iso_key_fast runs for the bigger graphs.

The formal version — 1-WL color refinement

Seed each vertex with its degree: \(c_0(v) = \deg(v)\). Iterate

\( c_{i+1}(v) \;=\; \mathrm{hash}\!\Big(c_i(v),\; \{\!\{\, c_i(u) : u \in N(v) \,\}\!\}\Big) \)

where \(\{\!\{\cdot\}\!\}\) is a multiset (so neighbour order is irrelevant — the implementation folds neighbour colors with a commutative sum-of-mixes). The partition refines monotonically and stabilizes in \(\le |V|\) rounds. The canonical key is the hash of the sorted multiset of stable colors. 1-WL is a sound (never merges non-isomorphic graphs that it distinguishes) but incomplete invariant — it fails on certain regular graphs — so for full safety the solver pairs it with individualization-refinement; for connected graphs on ≤4 vertices the sorted degree sequence is already a complete invariant, used as the TINY_MAX shortcut. The implementation pads neighbour lists to a fixed stride with a dummy vertex (mixed-color 0) so the inner loop is branchless and byte-identical to the variable-trip version.

6Sprague–Grundy: splitting a game into independent pieces

flip this section

Late in a game, the remaining available squares often split into separate clumps that cannot interfere with each other — a move in one clump never touches another clump. When that happens, you are really playing several tiny independent games at once.

Mathematics gives a beautiful way to combine independent games. Each game gets a number (its "nimber") that summarizes everything about it. To combine several games, you don't add their numbers — you combine them with a bitwise operation (XOR, the "nim-sum"). And there is one golden rule: the combined game is a loss for the mover exactly when the combined number is zero. This is the Sprague–Grundy theorem, and it is one of the jewels of game theory.

For this particular game it helps only at the very end (the queen board mostly stays in one connected piece because diagonals reach across everything), but where it applies, a clump of a few squares can be resolved instantly from a tiny lookup table instead of being searched.

When the available-graph splits into disconnected components, the position is a disjunctive sum of independent impartial games. By the Sprague–Grundy theorem each component has a nimber (Grundy value), and the position's nimber is their XOR (nim-sum); the position is a loss for the mover iff the nimber is 0.

\( \mathcal G(P) \;=\; \bigoplus_{c \in \text{components}} \mathcal G(c), \qquad \text{mover loses} \iff \mathcal G(P) = 0 \)

Each component's nimber is the mex (minimum excludant) of its children's nimbers:

\( \mathcal G(c) \;=\; \operatorname{mex}\{\, \mathcal G(c') : c' \text{ a child of } c \,\} \)

// nimber.rs — full Sprague-Grundy, no α-β cutoff (mex needs every child)
let mut seen = 0u64;                       // bitset of child nimbers (< n ≤ 16)
for &sq in &q.order {
    if q.is_available(blocked, sq) {
        seen |= 1u64 << self.grundy(q, q.place(blocked, sq));
    }
}
let mex = (!seen).trailing_zeros() as u8;  // smallest value not present

Two practical caveats specific to this game: (1) computing full nimbers has no α-β cutoff (mex needs the entire child set), so it costs ~265× the win/loss node count and is feasible only to ~n=12. (2) The queen graph is biconnected with long-range diagonals, so it does not decompose at the full-board level — Grundy decomposition is a near-dead-end on the trunk and pays off only in the fragmented endgame. The iso-window solver does exploit it at the leaves: a complete dense table of all labelled 8-vertex Node-Kayles games (and a ≤7 table) resolves every small subgame by one lookup (§7–8).

3Blue1Brown-style intuition · mex and XOR

mex ("minimum excludant") is just: look at the set of values your moves can reach, and name the smallest non-negative integer that's missing. That missing value is this position's nimber — it measures "how much maneuvering room you have." A nimber of 0 means every move hands the opponent a non-zero (winning-for-them) position — you're stuck.

Why XOR? Imagine each game as a stack of coins in binary. Playing optimally across several games is exactly the classic game of Nim, and the winning condition for Nim is "make the XOR of the pile sizes zero." Nimbers turn any impartial game into an equivalent Nim pile, so combining games is combining Nim piles — and Nim adds with XOR. A zero nim-sum is a balanced, lost-for-the-mover position; any non-zero sum, you can always rebalance it to zero and win.

The formal version — the Sprague–Grundy theorem

Theorem (Sprague 1935, Grundy 1939). Every impartial game under normal play is equivalent to a single Nim heap of size equal to its Grundy value \(\mathcal G\), where \(\mathcal G(P) = \operatorname{mex}\{\mathcal G(P') : P \to P'\}\) and \(\operatorname{mex}(S) = \min(\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\setminus S)\). For a disjunctive sum \(P = P_1 + \cdots + P_k\) (a move is a move in exactly one component), \(\mathcal G(P) = \mathcal G(P_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathcal G(P_k)\), where \(\oplus\) is bitwise XOR. The position is a P-position (mover loses) iff \(\mathcal G(P) = 0\). The proof of the XOR rule is the Nim strategy argument: from \(\mathcal G(P)\neq 0\) there is a move zeroing the nim-sum (clear the leading bit), and from \(\mathcal G(P)=0\) every move makes it non-zero — exactly the P/N alternation. Verified against OEIS A344227 through n=12.

7The solver lineage — getting progressively smarter

flip this section

Symmetry, graph shape, and game theory supplied the raw ideas. Making them coexist in one fast solver took several reversals. The first solvers searched the board directly. The middle generations learned that which positions are considered the same determines whether the problem fits in memory. The current generation goes further: for the most common small endgames, it is cheaper to recompute the exact answer from a tiny complete table than to remember the answer in a huge random-access one. Each generation below answers a bottleneck exposed by the previous one.

The Solver trait preserves a ladder of implementations (SOLVER_NAMES), simplest to most sophisticated. The lineage is not a list of unrelated optimizations: D₄ memoization exposed a capacity wall; graph-isomorphic keys reduced the set enough to fit but made each key expensive; a flat lockless TT removed the archive overhead; complete dense endgame tables removed the deepest re-expansion; and the recursive WK hierarchy removed the remaining hot random probes through pc=12. Every solver must still agree with memo-less naive on small boards.

The reversal that mattered

At one point the project looked stuck: roughly 36 million positions per second, with 16×16 hovering near 3m41s, and that number got written down as "the wall." It was wrong. The benchmark had run on a machine whose memory was already clogged — a full compressed-RAM swap, a disk cache eating 13 GB — so the big table didn't fit and every timing was garbage. Cleaning the machine, adding the dense lookup table, and forcing the memory system onto large pages took 16×16 to 2m44s. The takeaway became a standing rule: don't trust a "floor" until you've ruled out your own measurement.

A "~36 M/s floor, sub-50 unreachable, n=16 ~3m41s wall" was recorded — and was entirely an artifact. It came off a memory-degraded box (zram swap full, ZFS ARC eating ~13 GB, so the 17 GB TT OOM'd and runs were capped); it dismissed the W8 dense table on a single confounded capped run, and never tried forcing huge pages. On a clean box (swap off, ARC capped to 2 GB, /tmp tmpfs cleared) plus the W8 table plus MADV_COLLAPSE, the same solver finishes in 2m44s. The standing rule: treat any "floor" as a measurement artifact or an untried lever until proven otherwise — reason through the wall, not up to it.

Searching under a root — and where whole branches get pruned

From each root the solver opens out a game tree. It looks vast — but it almost never walks the whole thing. Four different mechanisms cut branches off before they're ever explored: it stops a branch the instant the outcome is decided, it reuses any position it has already solved (even a rotated or same-shaped one), and it reads the smallest endgames straight out of a table. 8Three orthogonal axes of optimization

flip this section

All the speed comes from three independent kinds of improvement. They multiply together, and it helps to keep them straight:

  1. Visit fewer positions. Remember what you've solved; merge look-alikes (symmetry, graph shape); resolve tiny endgames from a lookup table instead of searching them.
  2. Store each position more cheaply. Don't store the whole board; store a tiny "fingerprint" of it. Pack everything into 8 bytes. Carry a compact form down the search so you rarely touch the full board.
  3. Reach each stored position faster. This is the surprising one: the bottleneck is not thinking, it's waiting for memory. Each lookup is a random jump into a multi-gigabyte table, and the computer stalls waiting for it. Most of the cleverest tricks are about hiding or shortening that wait.

Three independent levers, each covered in its own section below. Most per-node micro-optimizations wash out (~0–5%); the levers that move the wall are node-count and memory/representation/latency. (A handful of micro-opts do pull their weight — §11 collects them.)

  1. Visit fewer nodes — memoization, D₄ canon, graph-iso canon, the complete W8 table, and the table-free W9–W12 evaluator hierarchy (never probe or expand pc≤12). (§3–8.)
  2. Represent nodes cheaper — 256-bit board as [u64;4]; the 8-byte TT slot with a 55-bit fingerprint; the in-band TinyGraph carried down so deep nodes never touch the 256-bit board. (§9.)
  3. Search them faster — lockless atomic TT, hash-carry, software prefetch, thread-local counters, huge pages, CPU affinity, const-generic monomorphisation, segmented indexing, and now sorted frontier waves. (§10.)

The W8 table — the lever that broke the wall

The single biggest recent win combines axes 1 and 3. Whenever the game shrinks to exactly 8 available squares, its outcome depends only on the shape of those 8 squares' conflicts — and there are few enough shapes that the solver can pre-compute the answer to every single one at startup, into a 32-megabyte table. So instead of searching that whole sub-game (and re-searching it thousands of times because it kept getting forgotten), the solver does one instant lookup and never expands it again. That alone cut the work by about 28%.

At popcount == 8 the available 8-vertex Node-Kayles subgame's value is isomorphism-invariant, so it is resolved by a single raw 28-bit labelled-edge-code lookup into a complete, eviction-free 32 MiB bitset (dense.rs, tables W0..W8 built bottom-up by vertex count). The win over plain iso-flat is less repeated work: iso-flat evicts pc==8 entries from the 13–17 GB flat table and re-expands their ≤7 subtrees repeatedly. W8 is complete ⇒ always a hit, no fingerprint, no eviction, TLB-friendly (16 huge pages). Measured ~28% fewer nodes (5.1 B vs 7.1 B).

// dense.rs — build all labelled Node-Kayles tables up to 8 vertices, bottom-up
for k in 1..=8 { tables.push(build_table(k, &tables)); }
// a row is the 28-bit upper-triangular edge code; value is relabelling-invariant
// Wk[code] = ∃ move i such that W_child[project(code, alive_after_i)] == loss

// iso_flat.rs — at the pc==8 node, build the code directly from the 8 attack rows

           else if pc <= 7      { !self.band_entry(...) }        // complete ≤7 table
           else                  { /* recurse */ };

The WINDOW flag is a const bool monomorphisation — plain iso-flat compiles to zero dead pc==8 branch, and iso-window builds the edge code in one pass straight from the 8 attack rows (no intermediate adjacency arrays). The ≤7 region is solved in a thread-private 128-byte L1 stack memo and stored in a complete labelled ~2 MB table — entirely off the DRAM path.

From W9 to W12 — compute the entry instead of storing it

The W8 table answers any 8-square endgame instantly. The obvious next step — a table for every 9-square endgame — is a trap: it would weigh about 8 gigabytes, and W10 would be vastly larger. iso-dense gets the same exact answers without materializing any of those tables.

When the game shrinks to exactly 9 available squares, every legal move places a queen, which removes that square and all the squares it conflicts with — leaving a smaller endgame of 8 or fewer squares. And those are exactly the ones the W8 table already knows by heart. So instead of searching the 9-square game, iso-dense just plays out each of its (at most nine) moves on paper, reads the resulting smaller endgame straight from the W8 table, and combines the answers: the 9-square position is a win the instant any one of its moves hands the opponent a losing endgame. Nine quick table reads, all from fast cache, and the entire sub-game — and everything beneath it — is decided without a single trip to the big 17-gigabyte table.

Then the recurrence is stacked: W10 asks W9, W11 asks W10, and W12 asks W11, with many moves jumping directly to smaller layers because a queen removes all of its neighbours too. Each layer replaces another band of random memory probes and all search beneath it with local arithmetic. The measured progression was 2m12s at W9 → 2m00s at W10 → 1m44s at W11 → 1m39s at W12. Subsequent tuning and run variance produced the current HEAD record: 1,926,031,300 nodes in 1m33s.

The trick · compute the table entry, don't store it

A complete 9-square table is too big to build or hold, but its entry can be derived from W8. That derived W9 value can serve W10, and so on. The hierarchy pays arithmetic to avoid random DRAM and subtree expansion. W13 found the stopping point: it removed another 15% of nodes but made n=16 about 4% slower. More pruning is not automatically more speed; K=12 is the measured economic optimum on this machine.

iso-dense extends the complete-dense idea through a recursive WK hierarchy. A complete labelled W9 would be 2^(9·8/2) = 2³⁶ rows ≈ 8 GiB, so it is never materialized. At popcount == 9 the value is computed on demand from W0..W8; W10..W12 apply the same exact one-ply recurrence over the evaluator below. W9 is the smallest and clearest instance:

// dense.rs — DenseW8::get9: exact value of one labelled 9-vertex graph,
//            with NO W9 allocation, canon, hash, or join.
let mut adj = [0u16; 9];

The kernel reads the 36-bit upper-triangular edge code, extracts each vertex's adjacency row with one _pext_u64 against a precomputed incident-edge mask (W9_MASKS.0[i]), then for each of the ≤9 legal moves forms the child alive-mask full & !(bit_i | adj_i) and projects the child's edge code with a second _pext_u64 against the child's induced-edge mask (W9_MASKS.1[child]). Because pext preserves the upper-triangle order of the surviving edges, the projected code directly indexes W[popcount(child)] — a complete table, so the lookup never misses. The node is a win iff any child reads back a loss; one short-circuiting OR-sweep, ~nine cache-resident bit reads, zero allocation, zero canonicalization, zero flat-TT traffic. A direct_w9_matches_scalar_recurrence test proves get9 matches the scalar negamax recurrence across thousands of codes.

In iso_flat.rs, wK_get builds the labelled graph code from the K live squares' attack rows and dispatches to get9…get13. The ceiling DK is a compile-time const generic, so iso-flat/iso-window compile the dense arms away and the K=12 default contains no K=13 arm. K≤11 fits in u64; K=12's 66-bit code is built as two u64 words and projected with a two-word pext. The runtime choice is resolved once at the subtree handoff, never per node.

Why this is a memory lever, and why it lands where the cost lives

A stratified TT-latency profiler first found that pc 9–12 carried ~64% of random-probe cost. The W9–W12 hierarchy now erases that whole region: pc≤12 resolves inline without a flat-TT get, put, or subtree expansion. The default W12 run cut about 60% of the W9-era node set and leaves the remaining probe cost concentrated at pc 13–21. Current record: 1,926,031,300 nodes in 1m33s.

Why this is not a generic "dense block." A naïve local solver over pc 9..K loses cross-boundary transposition merging and re-expands work (measured net-negative). iso-dense bottoms out in the complete, shared, eviction-free W0..W8 tables, so it performs an exact recurrence with no private boundary cache and no lost merge below its ceiling.

The crossover is measured. W9→W13 changed n=16 wall by roughly −22%, −13%, −10%, then +4%. W13 still removed ~15% of nodes, but its 78-bit wide projections cost more than the avoided pc=13 probe and expansion. Therefore K=12 is the n=16 optimum, while W13 remains a ready n=18 experiment where deeper reuse may amortize it differently.

9Representing positions cheaply

flip this section

A 16×16 board is 256 squares — too much to store billions of times over. Two tricks shrink it dramatically.

Store a fingerprint, not the board. Instead of writing down the full board, the solver records a short 55-bit "fingerprint" of it — like a hash of a document. If two boards land in the same memory slot and have the same fingerprint, they're treated as equal. Could two genuinely different boards accidentally share a fingerprint? Yes — but the odds are about 1 in 36 quadrillion per lookup, vanishingly small even across billions of lookups, and the final answer is cross-checked against the known result anyway. This shrinks each remembered entry to just 8 bytes — five times more entries per gigabyte than storing the board.

Carry a tiny form down the search. Once the game is down to a handful of squares, the solver stops touching the big 256-bit board at all and carries a tiny compact description instead — turning big board operations into single-byte operations.

The board is Bits([u64;4]) — 256 bits, four words. The transposition table stores not the key but an 8-byte slot: one u64 packing {used:1, val:8, fp:55}.

// tt.rs — the entire slot is one u64
fn pack(fp, val) -> Slot {
    Slot(1 | ((val as u64) << 1) | ((fp & FP_MASK) << 9))
}   //  bit0 = used, bits1..9 = value, bits9..64 = 55-bit fingerprint

The slot index already pins ~31 bits of the routing hash; the 55-bit fingerprint comes from an independent 64-bit hash half, so a wrong "hit" (a different key in the same slot whose fingerprint also matches) has probability ~2⁻⁵⁵ per colliding probe. This is the Chunk-2 win: 40 B → 8 B (5× the entries per byte) while keeping the available-mask key, so every transposition still merges exactly. A fingerprint mismatch is just a miss that re-searches; the only weakening is "wrong with vanishing probability," and the verdict is cross-checked against Jenrich.

The TinyGraph ([u8;8]×2, one cache line) is built once when a node drops to popcount ≤7 and carried down so the deep tail does single-byte alive & !closed[i] operations instead of 256-bit board ops. Hot structs follow Tiger-style discipline: plain data, contiguous storage, explicit #[repr], compile-time size_of asserts so a field-add can't silently re-pad.

3Blue1Brown-style intuition · why a 55-bit fingerprint is safe

It feels reckless to throw away the board and keep a hash. The intuition is the birthday paradox in reverse: a collision needs two keys to land in the same slot AND share all 55 fingerprint bits. The slot already separates keys by their routing bits, so within one slot you're asking 55 coin-flips to all match by chance — \(2^{-55}\), about one in 36 quadrillion. Even a Jenrich-scale 10¹¹ probes expects far less than one wrong hit, and one wrong hit would have to flip the final bit to matter — which the independent cross-check against the known verdict would catch.

The formal version — fingerprint collision probability

A wrong hit requires a foreign key \(k'\neq k\) to (i) route to the same slot and (ii) match the stored 55-bit fingerprint. Conditioned on a colliding probe (a slot already occupied by a different key), the fingerprint — an independent hash half — matches with probability \(p = 2^{-55}\). Over \(N\) such colliding probes the expected number of wrong hits is \(N\cdot 2^{-55}\); for \(N \sim 10^{11}\) (Jenrich scale) that is \(\approx 2.8\times10^{-6}\). Even unconditionally, the probability of any wrong hit is bounded by \(1 - (1-2^{-55})^{N} \approx N\cdot 2^{-55} \ll 1\). The error is one-sided in the sense that a fingerprint mismatch is always a correct miss; only a spurious match can mislead, and the global verdict is independently cross-checked against Jenrich's n=16 = second-player result.

10The current bottleneck: random memory plus frontend work

flip this section

Here is the counter-intuitive heart of the whole project. The computer is not bottlenecked on arithmetic. It's bottlenecked on waiting. Each position lookup is a random jump into a 17-gigabyte table, and a random memory jump is slow — the processor sits idle for the equivalent of hundreds of arithmetic operations while the data crawls back from RAM.

So the cleverest performance tricks are all about that wait:

  • One shared table, no locks. Many cores share one table with no "do not disturb" locks — safe because the same board always computes the same answer, so even if two cores write the same slot they write the same thing.
  • Look ahead. Before the solver needs a slot, it tells the processor "fetch this soon" (a prefetch), so the slow trip from RAM overlaps with other work instead of stalling.
  • Big memory pages. The processor keeps a small directory of where memory lives. A huge random-access table blows out that directory constantly. Forcing the memory into giant 2-megabyte pages shrinks the directory's job and cuts the stalls.
  • Count locally. Bumping a shared "positions searched" counter on every node would force the cores to constantly synchronize — so each core counts privately and reports in about once a second.

The solver reached this point by moving bottlenecks. Before W12 it was strongly DRAM-latency-bound: random probes into a 17 GB TT exposed roughly 120 ns each while using little bandwidth. On the current W12 workload, fresh TMA is co-dominant backend memory (~30%) and frontend (~31%); the dense evaluator removed billions of probes but added meaningful graph-code work. The mechanisms below are the landed latency controls, followed by the sorted-wave frontier that attacks both access order and duplicate work.

Lockless atomic transposition table

One flat Box<[AtomicU64]>, relaxed load/store, no mutex, no sharding. Safe by construction: a position's value is deterministic, so a concurrent write for the same key stores the same value, and a write for a different key is rejected by the fingerprint. This removes a lock/unlock and a mutex cache-line bounce from every node. (Hyatt's XOR-key trick is unnecessary — the 55-bit fingerprint self-validates identity.)

Lemire fastrange indexing

Map a 64-bit hash uniformly into [0, len) with one widening multiply + shift — no power-of-two table required, so the table can be sized to fill all available RAM:

\( \texttt{index}(h) \;=\; \big\lfloor (h \cdot \texttt{len}) / 2^{64} \big\rfloor \;=\; \texttt{(((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64)} \)

fn index(&self, route: u64) -> usize {
    ((route as u128).wrapping_mul(self.len as u128) >> 64) as usize
}

Hash-carry + software prefetch

Each key is hashed once at creation (hash128 → two independent halves (route, fp)); the halves are reused for the prefetch, the get, and the put. The solver issues _mm_prefetch on a child's slot before recursing, so the random-probe DRAM round-trip overlaps with the work in between.

Huge pages

A multi-GB table probed at random thrashes the dTLB on every node. MADV_HUGEPAGE alone only reaches ~73% 2 MB coverage on a randomly-faulted 17 GB table; MADV_POPULATE_WRITE + MADV_COLLAPSE forces 100% 2 MB — a clean ~5% wall win with near-identical node counts (so a genuine per-node TLB win, not noise). Default-on for ≥4 GB tables.

Thread-local counters + const-generic monomorphisation

The per-node node/HLL counter is thread-local, flushed ~once a second — a shared fetch_add per node bounced across the two CCXs over Infinity Fabric and measured a ~2× drag. And every run-constant toggle (measurement mode, window flag, key mode) is resolved once via a const generic at the call site, never a per-node branch — the search is frontend/L1i-bound, so a dead per-iteration branch would worsen the actual bottleneck.

3Blue1Brown-style intuition · the TLB / huge-page story

Think of RAM as a vast warehouse and the processor as a clerk with a small index card listing where the last few hundred shelves are. Normally that index covers tiny 4 KB shelves. When the clerk is fetching random items from a 17 GB warehouse, the shelf he wants is almost never on his card — so every fetch is two trips: one to look up the location, one to grab the item. Huge pages make each shelf 500× bigger (2 MB), so one index card now covers 500× more warehouse and the location is usually already on the card. Same items, far fewer double-trips.

After W12: one giant root, random probes, and sorted waves

Once W12 removed the deepest endgames, the shape of the remaining problem became unusually clear. Almost the entire solve sits under one hard opening move. Giving that branch more workers sounds natural, but it makes several workers rediscover the same positions. Five variants of finer splitting, restarting, coordination, and work stealing either broke even or got slower. The bottleneck is the amount and layout of work inside the giant root, not a shortage of scheduled tasks.

The remaining expensive lookups are concentrated in positions with 13–21 available squares. Today those lookups jump randomly around the giant table. A microbenchmark found that if the same destinations are sorted first, a few cores can read them as a stream about five times faster than today's one-at-a-time pattern. The active experiment therefore collects a frontier wave, removes duplicates, and probes it together. The next design moves sorting onto otherwise-idle cores so the searching cores receive ready-to-stream batches.

It remains transposition-safe because pc is a pure function of the key. The same property makes pc the only useful lossless chunking dimension for frontier waves; richer structural features have already been folded away by canonicalization.

Telemetry: turning a black-box solve into a measured system

flip this section

A final stopwatch time says whether a run was fast; it does not say why. The solver gradually acquired instruments that expose the search from several angles: how many different positions exist, which game stages dominate, which opening move owns the tail, where each core is spending time, and whether memory is slow because it is full, random, or contended.

Those instruments repeatedly changed the plan. Counting distinct positions justified graph-isomorphic keys and BuRR. Timing each opening exposed the single giant root. Counting work by remaining squares identified W8, then W9–W12. Per-stage memory timing showed that probes at 13–21 squares are still worth doing but need a better access order. Finally, a synthetic sorted-probe test showed that four streaming cores can outrun the current random-probe tail. Telemetry was not decoration added after the solver; it selected the solver.

All expensive instrumentation is gated and either monomorphised out of production or accumulated thread-locally and merged at drain. That constraint matters: an early shared per-node counter itself caused a ~2× cross-CCX regression. The observability design therefore follows the same hot-path discipline as the solver—no per-node locks, allocation, environment lookups, syscalls, or shared writes.

11Micro-optimizations: the small, sharp wins

flip this section

The big levers in this report — symmetry, graph shape, the lookup tables — are about doing less work. This section is about the opposite: the same work, done faster per step. In a search that mostly sits waiting for memory, most such tweaks change nothing you can measure. The job is finding the few that do. The trick is to ignore the stopwatch (the number of positions visited drifts run to run, so wall-clock is noisy) and instead watch two cleaner signals: how many machine instructions the processor needs per useful step, and how often it guesses a branch wrong and has to back up. A change that moves those moved something real. Here are the ones that did.

The search is memory-latency-bound, so a per-node arithmetic saving usually disappears into DRAM-stall noise. The optimizations below are the ones that survived a careful A/B. Two caveats on measuring them: (1) the node count varies run to run (eviction/scheduling), so wall-clock is the noisiest possible metric — the reliable signals are cycles-per-instruction and branch-miss rate, which are per-instruction ratios independent of how many nodes a run happens to walk; (2) benches are interleaved A/B (alternate the two binaries round by round), because this box thermally throttles ~1 s into a ~12 s n=14 solve, so all-A-then-all-B fabricates deltas. A change is kept only if it pulls its weight on those terms; the rest are reverted or recorded as negatives.

The standout · branchless move filter

At every position the solver walks its parent's move list and keeps the moves that are still legal. "Still legal?" is a near coin-flip deep in the tree, and a coin-flip is the worst thing you can ask a processor to predict — it guesses, misses about half the time, and stalls unwinding the wrong guess. The fix removes the question. Each candidate move is written down unconditionally, and the running count is nudged forward by a 0-or-1 — no "if." Same output, no gamble. Branch mispredictions fell about 34%, instructions-per-step about 9%, for roughly a 10% speedup.

Compacting the parent's move list to those still set in the child's avail was an if avail_has(sq) { buf[nc] = sq; nc += 1; } — a ~50/50 data-dependent branch the predictor misses every other node. Rewritten branchless: write the square unconditionally, then nc += avail_has8(avail, sq) as usize — an unavailable square is simply overwritten next iteration. The output slice is byte-identical (so the searched node set is unchanged), trading the misprediction for one always-taken L1 store. Measured ~34% fewer branch-misses, ~9% lower CPI, ~10% faster — the largest single per-node win in the kernel.

The rest, in brief — each a small, verified gain (or a structural choice that pays off many times over):

The remaining wins, with the mechanism and (where isolated) the measured effect:

8-byte fingerprint slot

Don't store the board — store a 55-bit fingerprint of it, the way a hash stands in for a document. A wrong match needs a different board to land in the same slot and match all 55 bits: about one chance in 36 quadrillion, and the final answer is cross-checked anyway. Each entry shrinks to 8 bytes.

Each TT slot is one u64 packing {used:1, val:8, fp:55}. The slot index already pins the routing bits; the 55-bit fingerprint comes from an independent hash half, so a foreign same-slot hit matches with probability ≈2⁻⁵⁵. Slot 40 B → 8 B = 5× more entries per byte, with the available-mask key intact so every transposition still merges.

Hash-carry

A position gets hashed once, when it's created, and that hash is reused for everything done to it: the look-ahead fetch, the lookup, and the later store. The old code re-hashed the same board three times.

hash128(key) yields two independent halves (route, fp) once per child at creation; the halves are threaded to the prefetch, the get_h, and the put_h instead of re-deriving them at each touch. Folds three hashes of a 256-bit key into one on the hot recursive path.

Software prefetch

Before the solver actually needs a table slot, it tells the processor "go fetch this now." The slow trip out to RAM then happens in the background, overlapping with other work, instead of freezing the processor when the data is finally needed.

Each child's slot is _mm_prefetch'd (hint T0) right after its key is hashed, before the recursive call. Because the search is random-probe-latency-bound (~120 ns/probe), issuing the prefetch ahead of the demand get overlaps the DRAM round-trip with the intervening work — memory-level parallelism on otherwise serial stalls.

Lockless atomic table

Every core shares one table with no locks at all. It's safe because a board's answer never changes: if two cores compute and store the same board, they store the same value, and a different board is caught by its fingerprint. No lock means no waiting in line.

One flat Box<[AtomicU64]>, relaxed load/store, no mutex, no sharding. Sound by construction: a position's value is deterministic, so a concurrent same-key write stores the same bits, and a different key is rejected by the fingerprint. Removes a lock/unlock and a mutex cache-line bounce from every node. (No Hyatt XOR-key needed — the fingerprint self-validates identity.)

Thread-local counters

Bumping a shared "positions searched" tally on every step would force the cores to keep handing one number back and forth. Instead each core counts on its own and reports in roughly once a second.

The per-node node/HLL counter increments a thread-local integer, flushed to the shared atomic only every ~2¹⁸ nodes (≈1 s). A shared fetch_add per node bounces the cache line across the two core complexes over Infinity Fabric — measured a ~2× throughput drag. The shared totals are made exact again by a drain at search end.

Huge pages

The processor keeps a tiny directory of where recently-used memory lives. A random-access table many gigabytes wide blows that directory out constantly. Forcing the table onto 2-megabyte pages makes each directory entry cover 500× more memory, so the lookups stop double-tripping.

A random-probed multi-GB table thrashes the dTLB. MADV_HUGEPAGE alone only reaches ~73% 2 MB coverage on a randomly-faulted 17 GB table; MADV_POPULATE_WRITE + MADV_COLLAPSE forces 100% — a clean ~5% wall win with near-identical node counts (a genuine per-node TLB win). Default-on for tables ≥4 GB.

Compile-time flag folding

Some switches are fixed for an entire run — which key scheme, whether a measurement counter is on. Rather than re-checking them at every step, they're baked into the code as constants when it's compiled, so the inner loop never even sees the question.

Run-constant toggles (measurement mode, window flag, key mode) are resolved once into const generics at the subtree-handoff site, so the hot loop has no per-node branch on them — dead arms are compiled away. The single runtime decision picks the monomorphised instantiation once. This matters because the kernel is frontend/L1i-bound: a dead per-iteration branch would feed the predictor garbage and worsen the actual bottleneck.

Multiply-shift indexing

Turning a hash into a table slot would normally use a remainder (division), which is slow and forces the table to be a power-of-two size. One widening multiply plus a shift does the same job and frees the table to be any size — so it can be sized to fill all the RAM available.

Lemire's fastrange: (((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64) maps a 64-bit hash uniformly into [0, len) with one widening multiply and a shift — no modulo, no power-of-two constraint. The table can be sized to any slot count, straddling the 17 GB→34 GB power-of-two gap on a 26 GB box. The extra multiply is free against the random-probe latency.

Carried orientations

The canonical "smallest view" of a board needs all 8 rotations and flips. Recomputing them from scratch at every step is expensive. Instead the solver keeps all 8 views and updates them as it descends — one quick bit-clear per move per view.

The 8 dihedral images of available are carried down the DFS and updated incrementally: placing a queen is one and_not against the move's attack mask in each orientation's frame (child[t] = parent[t] & !a[t]). The key is the lex-min of the 8. Measured ~62 cycles/canon vs ~574 for the from-scratch scatter — a ~9× cheaper key.

Eviction-proof small tables

Any position down to 8-or-fewer free squares is decided by a single lookup into a small table computed once at startup. Those answers can never be forgotten, so those whole sub-games are never searched twice — no matter how many different paths reach them.

The ≤7 region lives in a complete labelled table (one direct byte load, no fingerprint), and the W8 solver adds a complete 32 MiB table for all 8-vertex Node-Kayles games. Both are eviction-free, so a small subgame is always a hit — never re-expanded out of the evicting flat TT. The W8 table alone cut node count ~28% (5.1 B vs 7.1 B).

In-band compact graph

Once a position shrinks to a handful of squares, the solver stops touching the big 256-bit board entirely. It carries a tiny one-cache-line description and solves the whole little region inside a scratch memo that stays in the fastest cache — never reaching back out to main memory.

At popcount ≤7 a one-cache-line TinyGraph ([u8;8]×2) is built once; descendants operate on a u8 alive-mask (alive & !closed[i]) instead of four-word board ops. The whole subtree is solved in a thread-private 128-byte stack memo indexed by the alive mask — pure L1, no flat-TT probe, no DRAM, no cross-core coherence.

Serial early-out compare

Finding the "smallest" of the 8 board views means comparing them. The solver compares word by word and stops at the first word that differs — which is almost always the very first one, so most comparisons end immediately. A fancier branch-free version was actually slower.

lex_min8 folds the 8 images with a plain if cand < best; the Bits comparison is lexicographic over four u64 limbs and bails on the first differing limb. Most image pairs differ in word 0, so the compare exits after one limb — and this beat both a branchless lex_lt and a tree reduction in measurement.

Uninitialized scratch buffers

The move list is compacted into a small stack buffer. Zeroing a full board-width array first would cost more than the work it's helping — so the buffer is left uninitialized and only the entries actually written are ever read back.

The move-compaction target is a [MaybeUninit<u8>; MAXV] on the stack — no n²-wide zero-init (which would cost more than the scan it serves). The filter writes exactly the surviving prefix and returns that as &[u8]; MaybeUninit<u8> is layout-identical to u8, so reading back only the written prefix is sound.

The formal version — the 2⁻⁵⁵ fingerprint bound and the multiply-shift map

Fingerprint collisions. A wrong hit requires a foreign key \(k'\neq k\) to (i) route to the same slot and (ii) match the stored 55-bit fingerprint. Conditioned on a colliding probe, the fingerprint — an independent hash half — matches with probability \(p = 2^{-55}\). Over \(N\) colliding probes the expected wrong hits are \(N\cdot 2^{-55}\); at \(N\sim10^{11}\) (Jenrich scale) that is \(\approx 2.8\times10^{-6}\). A fingerprint mismatch is always a correct miss, so only a spurious match can mislead — and the global verdict is cross-checked against the known result.

Multiply-shift (Lemire). To map a uniform 64-bit hash \(h\) into \([0, \ell)\) without a modulo, take

\( \texttt{index}(h) \;=\; \Big\lfloor \dfrac{h \cdot \ell}{2^{64}} \Big\rfloor \;=\; \texttt{((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64}. \)

Reading \(h\) as a fraction \(h/2^{64}\in[0,1)\), this is \(\lfloor \ell\cdot(h/2^{64})\rfloor\) — the slot you'd get by scaling a uniform \([0,1)\) value into \([0,\ell)\). It is near-uniform for any \(\ell\) (the only deviation is the \(2^{-64}\)-scale rounding), so the table is freed from the power-of-two-size constraint that h & (len-1) imposes, at the cost of one full-width multiply instead of an and.

13How the speed was actually won: methodology and war-stories

flip this section

Everything so far has been the ideas. But the ideas were rarely the hard part. The hard part was measuring — on a small machine that turned out to be so touchy that, more than once, it faked a hard limit that simply wasn't there. A timing would look like a brick wall; clean up the machine, and the wall was gone.

What worked was a blend: a feel for where the time was probably going, knowledge of how the processor actually behaves, the right ideas from the research literature, and — above all — a refusal to trust a number until it had been re-checked under controlled conditions. This section tells that story: the toolkit, the instructive failures, and the working loop that produced the result.

The preceding sections are the levers. This one is the method that decided which levers were real. The recurring failure mode was never "we couldn't find a lever" — it was "we measured under a confound and believed the number." On a search that is memory-latency-bound and runs on a machine that swings ±2–3× with thermal state, page placement, cache pressure, and build flags, measurement hygiene mattered more than any single trick. The discipline below — pick the metric that doesn't move with the noise, isolate one variable, verify the fix flips the result — is what separated the ~10% wins that stuck from the dozens that were measurement artifacts.

The measurement toolkit

A handful of instruments did almost all the work. Together they answer three questions in order: what kind of stall dominates, where it lives, and whether a proposed fix actually helped.

  • Hardware-counter sampling. The processor keeps internal tallies — instructions run, cycles burned, branches guessed wrong, memory-directory misses. Reading these is the first move on any speed question, because some of them don't drift run to run the way the stopwatch does.
  • "Top-down" diagnosis. A single readout that splits wasted time into buckets: was the processor starved for instructions to run, mis-guessing branches, or just waiting on memory? It tells you which class of fix can possibly pay before you build anything.
  • Interleaved A/B runs. To compare two versions fairly on a machine that heats up and slows down within seconds, you alternate them turn by turn rather than running all of one then all of the other — otherwise the heat, not the code, decides the winner.
  • Statistical sizing. Before committing to a multi-hour run, a clever estimator counts roughly how many genuinely-different boards a search would face — without actually solving it — so you know in advance whether it will fit in memory.
  • Knowing which number to trust. The most repeated lesson. The headline stopwatch time is the noisiest signal here, because the count of boards visited wanders run to run. The trustworthy signals are ratios that don't depend on that count: work-per-step and wrong-guess rate. A change that moves those moved something real.

Each instrument, what it reads, and when it was reached for:

  • perf stat hardware counters — cycles, instructions (→ CPI/IPC), branch-misses, dTLB/iTLB-load-misses, L1d/L1i-load-misses, LLC misses. The first instrument on every question. Its decisive property: CPI and branch-miss-rate are node-count-independent, so one run resolves a per-node change that wall-clock can't — and the n=16 node count itself swings ±~18% run to run from parallel-cutoff timing, dwarfing most effects in wall time.
  • Top-down microarchitecture analysis (TMA) — attributes wasted pipeline slots to frontend-bound (instruction delivery: I-cache, iTLB, decode), backend-bound (execution: memory or ports), bad-speculation (mispredicts), or retiring (useful work). It decides the category of fix that can pay. The category shifted as the representation changed: the same program read as compute-bound (75% of cycles in the graph-key canonicalization at one stage), then DRAM-latency-bound (backend-by-memory ~34%, the hot annotation a test right after the TT slot load), then branch-mispredict-bound (branch-misses ≈ 24% of cycles, IPC ≈ 0.79) at successive stages.
  • dTLB / L1i / branch-miss budgets — used to bound a candidate before building it. In one round the counters said branch-misses cost ~22–24% of all cycles while dTLB misses cost ~7–8% even at a generous page-walk estimate, so the TLB lever was capped at single digits and the branch lever had ~3× the headroom. That is a back-of-envelope sizing call made straight from counters.
  • Interleaved (round-robin) A/B — the bottom-line keep/kill gate. Alternate the two binaries A/B/A/B, never all-A-then-all-B: the box thermally throttles ~1 s into a ~12 s n=14 solve, so a block design fabricates deltas. And at n=16, compare throughput (M/s), not wall — the node count is the noisy variable; throughput normalizes it.
  • HyperLogLog distinct counting — a cardinality estimator (~0.4% std err) fed every canonical key during a search, to size the problem before committing a multi-hour run. It put n=16 at ~7.2 B distinct under the cheap key (~2.1 B under graph-merge), which said a naive 8-byte-per-entry table can't fit but a ~1.1-bit/key compressed archive can — sizing the entire memory strategy without solving anything.
  • Which metric to trust — the throughline. Wall-clock is contaminated by throttle plus ±18% node noise; "nodes per second" is a trap (a colliding hash hit a higher rate but solved slower by manufacturing cheap nodes); the trustworthy per-node signals are CPI and branch-miss-rate. One run of each resolves a change the stopwatch can't.
The one rule

Watch the ratios that ignore how many boards a run happened to visit — work-per-step and wrong-guess rate — not the raw clock. On this machine the clock lies; the ratios don't.

On a throttled, latency-bound, parallel search, trust CPI and branch-miss-rate over wall-clock. They are per-instruction ratios independent of the ±18% node-count noise, so a single run resolves a per-node change the stopwatch can't — and they tie a measured effect to a mechanism (e.g. −9.4% CPI mechanistically consistent with −34% branch-misses).

A regression can contain the next breakthrough

Several of the largest eventual wins were slower in their first working form. The important question was not only “did the stopwatch improve?” but “did the experiment reveal a real prize underneath fixable overhead?” Graph-shape merging initially cost too much, the first flat solver was painfully slow, the W8 idea looked flat on a contaminated machine, and the explicit stack began 4.3% behind before reaching parity. The current ETC wave is the same kind of result: 9.2% slower, but it removes 17.4% of the search. That node cut is evidence worth optimizing, not a reason to stop at version one.

The project distinguishes a mechanism negative from an implementation negative. If the proposed causal quantity does not move—ABDADA did not reduce re-expansion, work stealing increased it, W13's extra node cut could not amortize wide projection—close the branch. If the causal quantity moves strongly but incidental cost dominates, optimize through it: graph iso delivered 3.4× merge while initially ~2.2× slow; iso-flat was eviction-free but WL-bound near 1 M/s before selective keying recovered >12 M/s; explicit-stack materialization was +4.3% before register/arena work reached parity; Phase-1 ETC is −17.4% nodes but +27% cycles/node. In each case the human had to push the agent past a premature “wall” or first-pass regression and demand separation of structural value from solvable implementation overhead.

Derive the floor; do not inherit it

The other human contribution was Elon-style first-principles thinking: estimate the work the answer fundamentally requires and what the machine can physically do, rather than treating the current program's roadblocks as natural law. If that estimate lies far below the stopwatch, the gap is an optimization budget—not evidence that the floor has been reached.

A defensible performance floor starts with irreducible state resolutions, minimum bytes moved, required arithmetic, and attainable memory/compute throughput. It does not come from extrapolating the current call graph, cache-miss profile, or most recent failed optimization. That distinction kept reopening the design: expensive live canonicalization became selective keying; random deep TT traffic became WK; and the remaining random probes are now being tested as sorted batches. Each “floor” described one implementation regime, not the problem.

A parallel case study: interactive transcript search, from hundreds of milliseconds to <100 µs

Before this solver, the same human–agent team built live search over a large archive of coding-session transcripts. The metric was the delay from a keystroke to the first visible results. With caches and prediction disabled, p99—the delay that 99 of 100 searches beat—fell from hundreds of milliseconds to below 100 microseconds.

That gain required many stacked changes, not one trick. In a representative round, a more selective filter rejected 2.6× more candidates but made the complete program slower. Measurement showed the filter itself was saving time; its rarely used metadata had merely disrupted the CPU-cache layout of frequently read data. Separating the two exposed the win: median latency fell 50% and p95 fell 83%.

The relevant lesson is about the division of labour. Coding agents could implement, instrument, and refine each idea, but repeatedly mistook a slow first version for a performance floor. Human persistence kept promising mechanisms alive; first-principles reasoning supplied a target below the roadblocks visible in the current implementation. At this level, the agent is a powerful engineering instrument, not yet an autonomous performance optimizer.

The comparison is methodological, not algorithmic. The cache-disabled benchmark forced a real index lookup on every keystroke and measured time to first result bytes. Reaching p99 <100 µs required compact indexes, early termination, off-critical-path publication, allocation-free buffers, hot/cold data separation, and careful worker placement.

The case-sensitive prefilter was a clean causal test: candidate count improved 2.6× and the filtering stage saved 69–119 µs, while end-to-end latency regressed because cold metadata disturbed the hot record's cache-line layout. Moving it to a sidecar produced p50 −50% and p95 −83%. The agent saw a regression; the human recognized a working mechanism buried under representation cost and insisted on the next experiment. Queens ETC now presents the same question. Current coding agents materially accelerate implementation and measurement, but human persistence and performance intuition still determine whether a local negative closes a branch or starts the optimization that makes it pay.

War-stories

Six episodes, each the same arc: a symptom, the obvious explanation that was wrong, the real cause, the fix, and the lesson that got written down so it wouldn't be re-learned.

Each card: symptom → the obvious-but-wrong reading → the real cause → the fix → the lesson. Chosen as the most instructive of the lot.

① The phantom speed limit that was really a clogged machine

Symptom. A long, careful optimization pass ended with a firm conclusion: about 36 million positions per second was the ceiling, 50 million was impossible on this hardware, and a 16×16 solve near 3m41s was the wall.

The obvious reading. The reasoning inside that pass was sound on its own terms — the time really was going to random memory waits, and squeezing the math harder genuinely wouldn't raise the rate. So the wall looked real.

The real cause. Three measurement faults stacked up. The machine's memory was already clogged — compressed-RAM swap full of stale pages, a disk cache eating ~13 GB, and around 11 GB of leftover profiling files sitting in RAM-backed scratch space — so the big table never fit and every "solve" ran crippled. A promising dense lookup table had been dismissed on one of these crippled runs. And nobody had forced the memory system onto large pages, so only about 73% of the table got the fast treatment.

The fix. Clean the machine until the table fit, add the dense lookup table, and force every page large. The same solver finished in 2m44s — comfortably under the goal that had just been declared unreachable.

The lesson. Don't trust a "floor" until you've ruled out your own measurement. The estimated best-possible time for this approach is actually under a minute, so 2m44s isn't close to any real limit.

Symptom. A "~36 M/s floor, sub-50 unreachable, n=16 ~3m41s wall" was recorded after an extensive, genuinely competent pass (affinity sweeps, TMA, MLP windowing, a small resident band table) that did bank a real +15% raw M/s and +10.6% n=14 completion — then concluded the wall was hard.

The obvious reading. Self-consistent on its terms: TMA said backend-by-memory ~34%, the high-popcount region is an unbounded random-DRAM probe stream, and node-reduction cuts completion but not the rate (M/s = nodes ÷ wall). It even proved the metric-trap directly — a 1 MB fingerprinted hash hit 63.5 M/s but solved slower (9.30 s vs 8.82 s).

The real cause: three stacked confounds. (1) A degraded box — zram swap full of cold pages from prior OOM storms, ZFS ARC eating ~13 GB, ~11 GB of stale perf.data pinned in tmpfs (= RAM) — so the 17 GB table was capped to 12–13 GB and never reached the eviction-free regime. (2) The complete dense W8 table was dismissed as a wash, but on the capped box and through a slow code path. (3) Huge pages were never forced — MADV_HUGEPAGE is advisory, and the fragmented box only promoted ~57–73% of the multi-GB table to 2 MB pages.

The fix. Clean the box (swap off, ARC capped, tmpfs cleared → ~24 GB free → the 17 GB table fit); rebuild the W8 hook as a one-pass lookup; add MADV_COLLAPSE + prefault for 100%-verified 2 MB coverage. Result: 3m41s → 2m44s. W8 alone won ~28% fewer nodes; collapse added a clean ~5% TLB win at near-identical node count.

The lesson, now a standing rule. Treat any "floor" as a measurement artifact or an untried lever until proven otherwise — reason through the wall, not up to it. The instruction-counted compute floor for this strategy is ~45–60 s, an order of magnitude under the claimed wall, which is exactly why the claim should have been suspect.

② The counter that cost 2× by bouncing between chiplets

Symptom. Two versions ran the identical core computation, yet one was twice as slow.

The obvious reading. "The machine regressed" — easy to blame the environment when the code looks the same.

The real cause. The slow version bumped a single shared "positions searched" counter on every position. This processor is built from two separate clusters of cores with their own local caches; a shared tally forces that one number to ricochet back and forth between the clusters across the chip's internal fabric, and every core ends up waiting on it.

The fix. Give each core its own private counter and have it report in only about once a second. That alone roughly doubled throughput. The same logic became a blanket rule: nothing shared, no settings lookups, and no memory allocation anywhere in the innermost loop.

The lesson. A "free" bookkeeping line on the hot path isn't free when many cores have to agree on it.

Symptom. Even given the identical kernel of a faster sibling, one solver ran 2× slower at n=14 — purely from the store path.

The obvious reading. "The box regressed" — the kernels matched, so the code looked exonerated.

The real cause. The HX 370 is two CCXs with separate L3s. A single shared atomic incremented every node by all 24 workers bounces over the Infinity Fabric; the per-node fetch_add on the node counter and the per-get HyperLogLog feed were both shared atomics on the hot path.

The fix. Move per-node accounting to a thread-local accumulator, flushed to atomics ~1×/s and drained once after the parallel search. That ~2×'d the solver and lifted every sibling that shared the path. It generalized to a rule: no atomics, no env-var reads, no syscalls (allocation included) in the inner loop — an env-var lock alone serializes every worker, the exact contention a lockless table removes.

The lesson. On a multi-CCX part, a per-node shared write is a cross-fabric coherence stall, not a cheap increment.

③ The gigabytes of ghost files poisoning every run

Symptom. The big table kept failing to fit or spilling, dragging every 16×16 timing down — the proximate cause behind story ①.

The obvious reading. A quick glance at free memory looked fine, so memory didn't seem to be the problem.

The real cause. Several invisible tenants were each eating RAM at once: cold pages stuck in compressed-RAM swap, a disk cache sitting at roughly half of memory, and — the surprise — about 11 GB of leftover profiling dumps in a scratch directory that is itself backed by RAM on this machine. A casual look at free memory never showed them.

The fix. A pre-flight checklist before any big run: swap off, disk cache capped low, caches dropped and memory compacted, scratch space cleared, and large pages forced. With that, ~24 GB came free and the table fit.

The lesson. A degraded machine silently fakes a wall; clean it before believing any number.

Symptom. The 17 GB TT OOM'd or spilled, capping every n=16 run — the proximate cause of story ①.

The obvious reading. A casual free -h glance looked adequate, so memory wasn't the suspect.

The real cause. With swap off and ARC capped, MemAvailable was still only ~12.9 GB; ~11 GB was pinned in tmpfs. du on /tmp found ~11 GB of stale *.perf.data from prior profiling — and /tmp is tmpfs (RAM) here, so disposable captures were pinning a third of memory, invisible to free -h and alongside zram cold pages and a ~50%-RAM ARC.

The fix. A pre-flight gate: swap/zram off; ARC capped low (~2 GB); drop_caches + compact_memory; clear /tmp; force 2 MB pages via MADV_COLLAPSE. With ≥~20 GB free the table fits eviction-free. (A subtlety: this box's "swap" is zstd-compressed RAM, not disk, so a spill is a per-access decompress cost, not disk I/O.)

The lesson. Box hygiene is a measurement precondition, not an afterthought; a starved box fakes a wall.

④ The profiler pointing at the wrong place entirely

Symptom. Hunting the source of mis-guessed branches, the profiler lit up one spot hard — 76% of the samples in a single helper — and it looked like the answer.

The obvious reading. Rewrite that helper; it's clearly the hotspot.

The real cause. Following the call chain, that helper turned out to be part of a one-time table the program builds at startup. The profiling run had been done on a short 14×14 solve — so short that the startup work dwarfed the actual search. The profiler had faithfully measured the setup, not the game.

The fix. Re-profile the real, long 16×16 run, where startup is a rounding error. The clean picture showed the mis-guesses spread thinly across the search, with one genuinely fixable spot — which became story ⑤.

The lesson. Profile the workload you actually care about, at full size; a short run measures your setup.

Symptom. Chasing the n=16 branch-miss source, a perf record -e branch-misses on a cheap n=14 run gave a sharp hotspot: 76% of branch-miss samples in a closure shim the compiler hadn't inlined.

The obvious reading. De-closure the hot iteration — the shim is the answer.

The real cause. Annotating with --call-graph dwarf, the caller chain placed the symbol inside a one-time OnceLock table build (a sort) — not the search. n=14 is so short that the startup build dominates the sample.

The fix. Re-profile the n=16 search with the record capped ~100 s post-startup. Clean profile: branch-misses diffuse across the kernel (inherent game-tree branching), with one concretely reducible branch standing out — the move-availability check (story ⑤).

The lesson. Localize on the production scale: at n=14 a perf record is swamped by the one-time table build that amortizes to nothing at n=16.

⑤ Deleting a coin-flip branch for a 10% win

Symptom. The counters showed the processor stalling badly — doing well under one useful instruction per cycle.

The obvious reading. "Everything's memory-bound, so there's nothing left to do but wait." Mostly true — but not entirely.

The real cause. At every position the solver walks its parent's move list and keeps the moves still legal. "Still legal?" is close to a coin-flip deep in the tree, and a coin-flip is the worst thing to ask a processor to predict: it guesses, misses about half the time, and pays to unwind the wrong guess. A budget estimate from the counters said these wrong guesses were eating far more time than the memory-directory misses everyone was focused on.

The fix. Remove the question. Write each candidate move down unconditionally and nudge the running count by a 0-or-1 — no "if." Same output, no gamble. Wrong guesses fell about 34%, work-per-step about 9%, for roughly a 10% speedup — beating the much fancier table-layout idea it was competing with.

The lesson. Even when "everything is memory-bound," a coin-flip branch can be worth removing — if the budget says wrong-guesses are large and a controlled test shows the cost wasn't already hidden behind the memory wait.

Symptom. n=16 IPC ≈ 0.79 — the pipeline stalling hard.

The obvious reading. Latency-bound, so bad-spec recovery is presumably hidden behind the TT-probe DRAM stalls and removing it buys nothing.

The real cause. A perf stat budget on a flat-vs-seg n=16 A/B (both ~8.5 T instructions): 143 B branch-misses at ~15–18 cyc ≈ 22–24% of all cycles, vs dTLB misses ~7–8% even at a generous ~75 cyc/page-walk. The one reducible branch (localized after fixing story ④) was the per-square if avail_has8(...) in the move filter — a ~50/50 coin-flip at every node. The hypothesis that the misses were latency-hidden was tested directly by the A/B, not assumed.

The fix. Branchless filter: write the square unconditionally, nc += avail as usize; byte-identical node set. Measured (normalized by node-count-independent CPI): branch-misses 143.5 B → 95.2 B (−33.7%), CPI 1.267 → 1.148 (−9.4%), IPC 0.79 → 0.87, wall ~205 s → 183 s (−11%). The −9.4% CPI is the trustworthy signal and is mechanistically consistent with −48 B misses × ~16 cyc. This single micro-opt (~10%) beat the whole segmented-TT lever (+5%).

The lesson. On a latency-bound search the trustworthy per-node metric is CPI / branch-miss-rate, not wall; and a per-node coin-flip is worth removing if the budget says bad-spec is large and the A/B shows it isn't latency-hidden.

⑥ Writing down what didn't work

Symptom. Plenty of plausible ideas that, when measured, made things worse or did nothing — and the temptation to quietly forget them and possibly try them again later.

The obvious reading. A failed idea is wasted effort; move on.

The real cause / the better view. A measured negative is a result. Several ideas looked clearly good on paper and were genuinely worth trying — a different proof-search algorithm, deeper parallelism, splitting the table across the two core clusters, comparing on raw throughput, an endgame shortcut — yet each lost in a controlled test, sometimes for subtle reasons (splitting the table across clusters does save a real ~13% on coherence, but loses more than that by re-searching positions the two halves no longer share).

The fix. Record each negative with its measurement and its reason, kept on a side branch rather than deleted. The next person reads the ledger and doesn't re-walk the dead end.

The lesson. "All cores at 100% in the monitor" is not the same as useful parallelism; and the cheapest speedup of all is not repeating a failure someone already paid for.

Symptom. A drawer of plausible levers that measured flat or negative, and the risk of someone re-trying them later.

The obvious reading. Discard the failures and move on.

The real cause / better view. A measured negative is a result worth keeping. The ledger: df-pn hits the proof-number/transposition pathology and explodes past n=8; the original per-node prove-loss MLP window lifted single-thread IPC 1.18 → 1.40 but added 19% instructions and regressed at 24 threads; CCX-partitioning saves ~13% coherence but loses ~42% cross-CCX sharing; a nimber oracle paid decomposition almost everywhere for ~1.7% hits; depth-preferred TT replacement measured 3× worse; and a colliding hash showed higher nodes/s while solving slower. The later sorted-frontier work is not a repeat of the old MLP window: it changes address order, exposes ETC cuts, and can offload preparation.

The fix. Record each negative with the exact mechanism it invalidates, not a broad label. That prevents repeating the same failed shape without incorrectly banning a materially different use of the underlying idea.

The lesson. "All cores at 100% in htop ≠ useful parallelism" (judge by wall + CPU-seconds); and a documented negative is a banked result.

How the collaboration worked

None of this came from one source. It came from a loop between a person and an automated assistant, run many times over.

The person set the direction and held the line — picking which lever to chase next, and flatly refusing to accept "we've hit the wall" as an answer (which, every time, turned out to be the right instinct). The assistant supplied the microarchitecture diagnosis and the candidate fixes: reading the processor's counters, proposing the branchless rewrite, the private counters, the large-page trick. And the research literature supplied proven building blocks — the game theory that guarantees the merging is sound, the estimator that sizes the problem, the compact data structures that fit billions of entries in a few gigabytes — so the wheel never had to be reinvented.

The thing that made it compound: every hard-won rule got written down. So the next pass didn't start from scratch — it started ahead, already knowing which walls were fake and which dead ends were dead.

The result came from a specific mix, iterated in a human + automated-assistant loop:

  • Human-guided intuition set the frame and refused the wall. Which lever to chase, when to clean the box, and the standing refusal to call any limit final — each rule the scar of a specific incident ("compare M/s not wall," "no atomics or env-vars in the inner loop," "focus on n=16, n=14 is validation only," "keep failed levers on branches"). The pivotal redirections — pinning to one core complex to crack the cross-CCX regression, cleaning memory in parallel during the floor overturn — came from that side of the loop.
  • Machine knowledge did the diagnosis and the fixes. TMA category reads, the branch-vs-dTLB budget call, catching the contaminated profile, the thread-local counter for cross-CCX coherence, compile-time monomorphisation to keep dead branches out of the frontend-bound loop, the branchless filter, MADV_COLLAPSE for full huge-page promotion — applied to whatever the tools surfaced.
  • Literature anchored correctness and direction. Correctness: the game is Node Kayles (PSPACE-completeness), verdicts cross-checked against OEIS A344227 and an independent 2014 n=16 computation. Direction: HyperLogLog to size the set, BuRR static retrieval for the ~1.1-bit/key archive, Lemire's fastrange for the index, external-memory delayed-duplicate detection for the bandwidth-bound dedup, Weisfeiler–Leman for the graph key.
  • Every rule was written down. The validation gates and the recorded negatives are the shared memory that let each pass start ahead of the last — including the explicit dead-end ledger, so no one re-walked df-pn, MLP, or CCX-partitioning.

The throughline: the bottleneck was almost never where the first measurement said it was, and the first measurement was almost always taken under a confound. Progress came from distrusting the headline number, isolating one variable, verifying the flip, and refusing to call any wall final.

Transpositions are the whole game. Below is a tiny illustration of why remembering positions matters so much: two completely different sequences of moves can arrive at the exact same board. Without memory, the solver would explore that board (and everything beneath it) twice. With billions of positions and many paths into each, this duplication is the difference between minutes and years.

The search graph is a DAG, not a tree: many move-orders converge on the same blocked mask (transpositions are strictly intra-ply — each move places one queen, so transposing positions share a queen count). The TT turns the exponential tree into the polynomial-in-distinct-positions DAG. The figure shows the smallest non-trivial case.

Two horizons, not one vague “next optimization”

Sub-minute 16×16 is a locality problem inside the current algorithm. W12 already removed the small endgames; more workers and W13 both measured worse. The live bet is to gather the remaining 13–21-square positions into batches, sort them by where they live in memory, remove duplicates, and let a few cores read them as a stream. A small in-search version is being tested first. If it pays, idle cores will prepare those batches while the busy cores search.

18×18 is a capacity and algorithm-shape problem. Its estimated trillions of positions cannot live in the current flat RAM table. The likely route breaks depth-first search into layers: generate a layer, sort and deduplicate it, solve each distinct position once, then freeze the answers into a compact BuRR archive on disk before moving upward. That reintroduces BuRR in a role where it fits—immutable completed layers—not in the live search.

And the deepest open question: is there a clever theorem for even-sized boards (like the instant mirror-strategy for odd boards) that would make the search unnecessary entirely? None is known. Finding one would beat every silicon trick at once.

Track A — sub-minute n=16

  1. Phase 1a, measured baseline: in-DFS ETC + sorted-batch child probes (QUEENS_WAVE) removed 17.4% of n=16 nodes but added 9.2% wall / 27% cycles per remaining node. Earlier transposition cutoff is large and real; the first implementation pays too much to find it.
  2. Phase 1b, immediate next: optimize the new code before changing architecture. Fuse gather with the normal child loop, carry hashes/probe outcomes forward, avoid rebuilding keys and double-probing no-cut children, shrink scratch/stack traffic, and determine whether sorting tiny per-node batches pays. Then rerun the identical three-plus-round interleaved n=16 A/B.
  3. Phase 2, conditional: if optimized Phase 1 remains wall-negative, move preparation off-path with an idle-core producer / few-consumer pipeline. Producers gather a pc-band frontier, sort, deduplicate, and pack SoA chunks; roughly four consumers stream near the measured ~780 M probes/s cap.
  4. Gate: lineage agreement, fresh n=16 SECOND, and interleaved A/B on wall, cycles, nodes, and cycles/node. The −17.4% node cut is the opportunity; only the remeasurement decides the architecture.

The target is defensible but not guaranteed. The sorted microbenchmark bounds only the memory portion: sorting, frontier materialization, freshness re-probes, and lost DFS residence all reduce realization. The current 1m33s record leaves 33 seconds to remove; the measured exposed-memory prize alone is closer to 10–15%, so sub-minute likely also needs dedup/node reduction or frontend improvement.

Track B — attempting n=18

  1. Break DFS residence: process pc/ply windows as DDD waves; external-sort by key, deduplicate globally, and solve each boundary graph once.
  2. Freeze completed layers: use value-only BuRR (~1.1 bits/key) when membership is known from the generated frontier. This is where BuRR returns; it is absent from the current live iso-dense search.
  3. Re-evaluate the dense ceiling: W13 is −15% nodes but +4% wall at n=16; n=18 may amortize the wide evaluator differently. Measure rather than assume.
  4. Make verification a first-class deliverable: there is no independent n=18 verdict. Reproducible layer hashes/counts, redundant runs or implementations, checkpointing, and deterministic frozen artifacts matter as much as wall time.

The estimated n=18 set is on the order of 3 trillion positions. A compressed iso-keyed archive may be on the order of 130 GB, but that is a sizing estimate. The decisive unknown is whether win/loss cutoffs can be preserved well enough in a layer-oriented computation; full retrograde materialization without them could erase the storage win.

The outsized theoretical lever remains an even-n pairing theorem analogous to the odd-n O(1) result. None is known; PSPACE-completeness of generalized Node Kayles does not rule one out for this board family.

Appendix: iso-dense, end to end

This appendix is deliberately self-contained. It describes the current K=12 default as an algorithm, then explains why each layer exists and which earlier mechanism it replaces. The short version is: search large positions with a flat D₄-keyed TT; never send positions with 12 or fewer available squares to that TT; evaluate them exactly as small Node-Kayles graphs; parallelize only where the boolean cutoff makes parallel work non-speculative.

A. The state and recurrence

A position is represented by a 256-bit available mask. Placing a queen at square s removes s plus every available square attacked by it:

child = available & !attack[s]

Nothing else about move history matters. Therefore distinct move orders that produce the same available mask are one state—a transposition. The solver needs one boolean recurrence:

\(W(P)=\exists m\in moves(P):\neg W(P_m)\)

An empty position is losing for the mover. A node is winning as soon as one losing child is found; that is the cutoff that makes move order valuable and uncontrolled parallelism dangerous.

B. What is carried down the search

For large positions, iso-dense carries all eight D₄ orientations of the available mask. A child updates those orientations incrementally instead of rotating/reflection-folding a fresh 256-bit mask. The lexicographically smallest orientation is the TT key. It also carries a filtered move list: because availability only shrinks, each child filters its parent's already-short list rather than scanning all 256 squares.

This is one place where the name can mislead. The current champion does not run a graph-isomorphism canonicalizer in its live hot path. That was the key idea in iso-burr and iso-flat. With DK=12, every position small enough for the selective graph-iso band is intercepted by WK first; positions above the dense ceiling use the cheaper D₄ key.

C. The flat transposition table for pc ≥ 13

The live TT is one flat array of 8-byte atomic slots. A 128-bit hash is split into an indexing half (route) and an independent 55-bit fingerprint plus value. A probe is one relaxed atomic load; a put is one relaxed store. Same-key races are benign because the game value is deterministic, while a foreign key must collide in both slot and fingerprint to look like a hit.

The table is prefaulted and collapsed into 2 MiB huge pages. It has no locks, per-worker shards, or BuRR segments. That last point is the decisive difference from the compressed branch:

D. W0..W8: a complete, eviction-proof base

At pc≤8 the geometry of the board no longer matters once the conflict graph is built. Label the live squares 0..k−1 and encode the upper triangle of their adjacency matrix. For k=8 that is 8·7/2 = 28 bits, so every labelled graph indexes a bit in W8. Tables W0 through W8 are built bottom-up once and occupy about 32 MiB in total.

W[k][graph] = any vertex v such that
              W[child induced by graph - N[v]] == LOSS

The table is complete, not a cache: there is no fingerprint, collision, eviction, canonicalization, or miss. Graph values are invariant under relabelling, so any deterministic ordering of live squares is valid. This removed the repeated ≤8 subtree expansion that a flat TT could forget.

E. W9..W12: exact tables that are never materialized

A literal W9 bitset would require 2³⁶ bits—8 GiB—and W10 would require 2⁴⁵ bits. Instead, getK computes only the requested row of the conceptual table. For each possible move it removes the chosen vertex and its neighbors, projects the surviving induced graph, and calls the lower exact layer:

getK(code):
    adjacency = extract_rows(code)
    for vertex v in 0..K:
        alive = all_vertices - v - neighbors(v)
        child_code = pext(code, induced_edge_mask[alive])
        if lower_layer(popcount(alive), child_code) == LOSS:
            return WIN
    return LOSS

BMI2 pext is the enabling primitive: given a precomputed mask for the surviving vertices, it gathers exactly those edge bits while preserving their order. The result directly indexes W≤8 or feeds get9, get10, or get11. There is no heap allocation, graph canonicalization, hash, TT probe, or join.

F. The hot-path dispatch

The production loop evaluates children in this order:

pc = popcount(child)
if pc == 0:                    parent wins immediately
else if pc == 12:              lost = !get12(graph_code(child))
else if pc == 11:              lost = !get11(graph_code(child))
else if pc == 10:              lost = !get10(graph_code(child))
else if pc == 9:               lost = !get9(graph_code(child))
else if pc <= 8:               lost = !W[pc][graph_code(child)]
else:                          lost = !wins_inc(child)  // flat TT + DFS
if lost:                       parent wins; stop siblings

DK is a const generic chosen once at the root/subtree handoff. The K=12 binary does not execute a runtime “which dense ceiling?” branch per node, and its hot body contains no W13 arm. Measurement modes—histogram, segmented index, profiler, wave—are selected at the same handoff into separate monomorphisations.

G. Parallelism: where it is sound and where it stopped paying

Symmetry-distinct opening roots are distributed across Rayon workers sharing the TT. Within a root, only prove-loss levels—where every child must be refuted—fan out without speculation. Prove-win levels remain ordered and sequential so the first losing child can cut off its siblings.

This creates a tail dominated by one opening root, but attempts to fill idle cores by splitting it changed the work: finer splitting, ABDADA deferral, warm restart, and frontier work stealing increased or merely rearranged concurrent re-expansion. The best tuned work stealing was +8.7% nodes and +13.3% wall. iso-dense therefore accepts imperfect utilization when preserving transpositions and cutoff order is cheaper. Its default warm-restart phase is retained for a small node trim but is wall-neutral.

H. Why the current sorted-wave experiment follows naturally

After W12, 87% of remaining TT probe cost is at pc=13..21. Those probes have enough hit value that skipping them is ruinous, yet their random order exposes DRAM latency. The gated QUEENS_WAVE path performs enhanced transposition cutoff: gather up to 32 recurse-arm children, sort them by route/slot, issue all prefetches, then probe the batch before expanding any child. A cached losing child proves the parent immediately; otherwise normal DFS continues unchanged.

This is correctness-neutral—it only discovers an existing cutoff earlier—and off-mode is byte-identical. The definitive three-round n=16 A/B cut nodes 17.4% but increased cycles 4.9%, wall 9.2%, and cycles/node 27%. Gather, child-key rebuild, double cold probes, sorting, and scratch traffic currently cost more than the skipped expansions. Before drawing an architectural conclusion, the next pass will remove that redundancy and remeasure. If the optimized path still loses, the same 17.4% cut becomes the quantified prize for off-critical-path preparation.

I. Correctness and trust boundary

  • Every dense evaluator is compared with a scalar recurrence over thousands of graph codes.
  • All solver variants agree with memo-less naive on small boards.
  • The iso-flat control reproduces the exact n=12 distinct count, 1,060,823.
  • Behavior-changing full-scale experiments must return n=16 SECOND.
  • The final n=16 verdict is independently cross-checked against Jenrich's 2014 71.46-billion-call computation.

The 55-bit TT fingerprint makes a false hit probabilistic, but the independent verdict and solver/kernel cross-checks make that risk observable at multiple levels. For n=18, where no independent verdict exists, frozen-layer hashes and redundant computation must replace that external safety net.

It could freeze known keys into roughly 1.1 bits/value-key and verify them, providing the first credible route around flat-TT eviction. Root-set and branching telemetry measured cross-root reuse and the proof-DAG gap; theoretical-floor and n=18 notes sized future storage.

Kernel progress. The incremental solver (1b8bdcd) carried orientations and attacked state down the tree, improving n=14 by about 1.5× at the same node set. Checkpoint UX and PGO were aligned with that path.

Live BuRR branch. burr made BuRR the primary live LSM store (8edc10a), with prefetched Bloom filters and shared hashing. The first design froze monolithically and OOMed; append-only segments, per-segment Blooms, and a hard memory cap (64d86db) made it survive full scale.

First project n=16 finish. Combining graph-isomorphic keys with the eviction-free archive produced iso-burr: SECOND in 29m23s. Capacity was proven solved and Jenrich's verdict independently matched.

Roadblock exposed. The graph key itself was initially ~2.2× underwater, despite its 3.4× merge. More importantly, live BuRR misses walked a growing cascade of immutable segments, so throughput fell as the search progressed. The first complete solution therefore identified the next bottleneck: lookup shape, not storage volume.

Wednesday, June 17 — drop the compressed live store; make every lookup O(1)

Combination that lost. fused tried to combine incremental state, graph-isomorphic keys, and live BuRR freezing. It finished n=16 in 38m26s—slower than iso-burr—because redundant keying and the segment cascade outweighed the fusion.

Measurement confound. Several n=14 regressions were initially blamed on code, then traced to machine state and shared counters. The TT huge-page advice was also silently failing with EINVAL on a misaligned interior pointer until b3bc58f. Thread-local hot counters and explicit CPU affinity removed coherence and scheduling noise. This episode produced the pre-flight and interleaved-A/B discipline used afterward.

Architectural reset. iso-flat (241000f) kept the useful selective key but dropped BuRR and the LSM cascade for one flat lockless TT. Initially it fit but ran near 1 M node/s because live Weisfeiler–Leman canonicalization was too expensive. Restricting graph-iso to the tiny, deep band and recovering the fused kernel raised sustained throughput above 12 M/s (9331f9b).

Rapid milestones. n=16 fell to 9m11s, then 8m20s with KEY_MAX=7, then 7m02s with TT sizing. Hash carry, tighter keys, branch/work reductions, and a serial hot-path rewrite brought the full result to 4m11s. An incremental move-list change cut instructions 25% yet lost 5.7% wall—an early warning that instruction count alone does not predict a latency-bound solve.

End-of-day model. Flat lookup was the right live store. The remaining waste was deep small positions being evicted from a multi-gigabyte cache and recomputed.

Thursday, June 18 — make the deep tail complete and overturn a false floor

Tail extraction. pc≤7 became a compact local graph game carried without the 256-bit board, first in L1 memo and then in a complete direct labelled table. The best version deliberately avoided canonicalization: direct labelled indexing was ~15% faster even though it merged less, because the complete table never misses.

Measured negatives. Per-node MLP windows increased instructions enough to erase latency overlap; an L2 fingerprinted hash was worse than the direct 2 MiB table; CCX partitioning saved a real coherence cost but lost more through cross-CCX re-search. These results narrowed the viable tail design to complete shared values.

W8 breakthrough. iso-window precomputed every labelled Node-Kayles value through 8 vertices and terminated the search at pc=8. On a clean box with forced 2 MiB collapse, n=16 reached 2m44s (2457646). This overturned a recently declared ~3m41 “floor,” which had been measured with full zram, a 13 GB ARC, stale tmpfs profiles, a capped TT, and incomplete huge-page promotion.

Instrumentation and kernel work. Per-pc write histograms and a segmented TT were added; a branchless availability filter cut branch misses ~34% and cycles about 10%. Together segmentation and the filter gained roughly 16% throughput. Set-associative bands were only break-even.

Large theoretical win that lost. A component-nimber oracle cut nodes up to 74% but made wall time 6.6× worse because mex requires all children and WL/nimber recursion dominated. Dense-nimber coverage telemetry then killed the proposed salvage. The lesson became explicit: a node saved is worth only the cost of the node it replaces.

Friday, June 19 — locate the hot pc bands and replace them with WK

Fresh attribution. Top-down measurements found co-dominant memory and frontend/i-cache cost; return misprediction was effectively zero, so recursion removal itself had no prize. Collapsing a duplicated PROVE_LOSS monomorph cut i-cache MPKI 74%.

False start that revealed the answer. A generic dense-block solver was net-negative because local boundary work lost transposition sharing. The new QUEENS_PROF stratified timer then corrected the initial interpretation: 63.5% of random-probe cost was at pc 9–12, not the upper tree. The block failed on per-resolution compute, not lack of coverage.

WK breakthrough. The key insight was to compute a conceptual dense-table row without storing the table and without introducing a private boundary. W9 (bcb70e6) used BMI2 projection into complete W0..W8 and reached 2m12s. The generalized hierarchy reached W11 / 1m44s (7f7bbf9) and W12 / 1m39s (84800a2). W12 was the first 66-bit two-word evaluator.

Crossover measured, not assumed. W13 (fc3865f) removed another ~15% of n=16 nodes but regressed wall ~4%; K=12 became the default optimum. Building the wide code as two u64 words cut w12_get's own cycles. W12 also reduced TT occupancy because pc≤12 is never stored.

Parallelism branch closes. Root/utilization measurements showed the giant-root tail, but finer split/taper added re-expansion and warm restart was wall-neutral. A recursive-to-explicit-stack conversion initially cost 4.3%, then was optimized to parity; it was retained only as infrastructure for frontier experiments.

Saturday, June 20 — stop scheduling duplicate work; reorder it

Coordination experiments. ABDADA in-flight markers were implemented and validated (773ba8d) but could not profitably defer the large pc 13–21 nodes. Frontier work stealing then published even-depth children to idle cores. After tail gating and extensive tuning, the best variant still added 8.7% nodes and 13.3% wall. This was the fifth failed attempt to extract more DFS-local parallelism.

Re-anchor on the actual champion. Fresh W12 root timing showed one root lasting ~94% of wall and only a 17.5-second solo tail; the scheduling ceiling was therefore small. TMA remained co-dominant memory (~30%) and frontend (~31%). The best observed default run was 1,926,031,300 nodes in 1m33s.

Probe economics. pc 13–21 carried ~87% of remaining get cost, each around 176–180 cycles with 34–39% hit rates. Skipping the checks was refuted by about 22× expected value and independently by W13's wall regression.

Current breakthrough. A random-versus-sorted TT microbenchmark found that sorting by slot changes the hardware regime: one-core sorted depth-32 reached 274.7 M probes/s versus 48.6 M/s random depth-1; four sorted consumers reached 763.8 M/s and the subsystem saturated near 780 M/s. The advantage survived contention and was largest with few consumers—the exact shape needed to use idle cores for sorting rather than duplicate searching.

Current work stream. A″ Phase 1a is committed at e80cfc9: −17.4% nodes, +9.2% wall. Phase 1b now optimizes that code and remeasures; it is too early to declare the in-DFS shape closed. If its redundant work cannot be removed far enough, Phase 2 moves gather/sort/dedup to idle-core producers. Phase 3 remains a separate ply-windowed retrograde DDD/BuRR architecture for n=18.

The cumulative lesson

The six-day progression was not additive feature accumulation. The champion was produced by repeatedly dropping a mechanism after it had taught the next lesson: BuRR live storage proved compression but was replaced by a flat TT; live graph-isomorphic hashing proved the right equivalence but is bypassed by WK in today's hot tail; recursive-stack conversion enabled frontier experiments but provided no speed itself; parallel coordination exposed transposition saturation and yielded to sorted/deduplicated preparation.

A second pattern is just as important: many real wins arrived disguised as first-pass regressions. Graph iso, iso-flat, W8, and the explicit stack all needed another implementation round before their structural value could be judged. Coding agents accelerated implementation and measurement, but repeatedly treated a slow prototype—or one mechanism's limit—as “the floor.” Human persistence kept the work moving; Elon-style first-principles reasoning supplied the deeper intuition, deriving the plausible floor from irreducible work and hardware capability rather than from current roadblocks. That made the right question: did the merge, node cut, locality change, or representation work, and what overhead remains removable? Phase-1 ETC is the latest case—wall-negative today, but with a 17.4% node cut large enough to demand an optimize-and-remeasure pass. At this frontier, the agents are powerful tools; they do not yet reliably push performance to the endpoint without human judgment.
