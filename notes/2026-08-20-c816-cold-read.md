## 2. The read-through: where I got lost

### 2.1 First breakdown — the abstract, sentence 3 (p. 1)

> "After an ordering, chart lift, outer labels, and Petersen labels are fixed as a marked bridge
> datum, the two sheets are labelled by an order-six conference source and its opposite; the
> sheet alone supplies none of that marking."

Reading cold I could not tell what is being claimed. Four undefined nouns ("chart lift", "outer
labels", "Petersen labels", "conference source") are bundled into a fifth ("marked bridge datum"),
and the sentence's actual content — that the labelling is *not* determined by the geometry — is
delivered as a subordinate clause. I had to read the whole paper and come back. An abstract that
requires the paper is not doing its job.

The final abstract sentence is the same problem in miniature: "a relative sign comparison between
cubics on different spaces, not an identification of their ambient harmonic representations." On a
cold read this parses as a retraction of the sentence before it.

### 2.2 Second breakdown, and the real one — Proposition 1.2 (pp. 3–4)

This is where my understanding actually failed rather than merely slowed.

Proposition 1.2 is stated on p. 3 and uses, in its statement: `ιₜ` ("defined in Section 2"),
"Hitchin's incidence normalization", `B₊`, `B₋`, "the fixed isotropic plane", "incidence deck
exchange", "an odd generator normalized on `B₊`", "Hitchin's degree-ten degeneracy locus (defined
in Section 2.2)", and `σ₃`. Of these, `Uₜ` — the isotropic plane that "selects `B₊`" and therefore
carries the entire sign convention the paper is about — is not defined until p. 7.

The consequence is not cosmetic. Proposition 1.2 *is* the paper's bridge; it is the object the
title's "golden descent" refers to. Placing it where its vocabulary does not yet exist means the
reader's first encounter with the paper's central claim is with a statement they cannot evaluate.
I had to read §§2.1–3.3 and then reconstruct Proposition 1.2 from scratch. When I did, it held up.
But a referee who does not do that work will form their opinion of the paper on p. 3.

Related: "For an odd generator normalized on `B₊`, their equations are `z = 4√5σ₃`, `z = −4√5σ₃`."
The number `4√5` is unexplained at this point. Its explanation — `√(5·16) = 4√5`, the product of
the descent class and the chart factor — arrives on p. 11 and is the single most illuminating
sentence in the geometric half of the paper. It should be on p. 3.

### 2.3 Third: §2.1's normalization of `J₀` (pp. 5–6)

See §4.2 below. I reread this passage four times before concluding that the constant 16 is a free
choice rather than a datum imported from Hitchin, and that the paper's own logic requires only
that it be *a rational square*. The passage says both things.

### 2.4 Fourth: the outer six-set (p. 15)

`T` is introduced as "the six synthematic totals, that is, the six labelled one-factorizations of
the fixed labelled graph `K_X`. These are the classical mystic pentagons. For each `T ∈ T`, its
complementary triangle colouring assigns signs `T(S) ∈ {±1}`…"

The map from a one-factorization to a signed triangle colouring is never given. It is cited to
[14, §§1.1–1.2, 1.6]. Everything in §5.1 — the whole operator stage, which the introduction calls
"the operator stage on the main route" — is built on an object whose definition lives in another
paper. That is heavier work than a citation should carry, and it is the only place in the paper
where I could not proceed without leaving it.

Compounding this: the paper takes `T_r = p_r T₀` for six explicit permutations of `{3,4,5}` and
calls the resulting six rows "the six outer coordinates", without observing that the six rows of
table (3) are pairwise distinct and that distinctness is what makes them the whole orbit. It is a
one-clause fix. (They are distinct; I checked.)

### 2.5 Fifth: "balanced" means two unrelated things (pp. 22–26)

In §5.2 a "balanced half `Y`" is never defined. It first appears mid-sentence on p. 23 and must be
inferred from context to mean a `d`-subset of the `2d` indices. Then in §5.3 "the three balanced
cuts `B₁₂ = 1100`, `B₁₃ = 1010`, `B₁₄ = 0110`" uses "balanced" for a weight-two vector in `F₂⁴`,
an unrelated notion, four pages later in the same section. I lost time reconciling them.

### 2.6 Smaller places I had to reconstruct

- **§6.1, "Requiring the comparison to preserve `σ₃` forces the scalar to have cube one."**
  As written, the comparison goes from `V` to the Petersen coefficient module, and `σ₃` lives only
  on `V`, so there is nothing for it to preserve. The intended statement is about the composite
  self-map of `V` through the inverse `yᵢ = ⅓Σ_{j≠i}a_{ij}`. Say so.
- **§6, why the cubic integral is `A₅`-invariant.** The paper establishes that the labelling of the
  ten axes matches the Petersen graph via the two inner-product values. It does not say that every
  `A₅` permutation of the five labels is induced by a *rotation* — which is what makes the integral
  invariant. (It follows: the icosahedral rotation group acts faithfully on the ten face axes, so
  it embeds in `Aut(Petersen) = S₅` as an index-two subgroup, hence as `A₅`. One sentence.)
- **§6.1, "The invariant cubic line is generated by `σ₃`."** Asserted. It is true —
  `dim(Sym³V₄)^{A₅} = 1`, which I verified by character — but the whole of Theorem 6.1 rests on it,
  since the theorem is proved from a single evaluation.
- **§2.2, "Let `π : I → P(H)` be the first projection. It is a projective bundle over `X`."**
  The antecedent of "It" is `I`, not `π`. Trivially fixable, but it is the kind of slip that costs
  a reader thirty seconds at exactly the wrong moment.
- **Theorem 5.2's last clause**, "relative to an orientation of the signed label space, the sign is
  that space's two-valued orientation character," uses a term ("the signed label space") that
  appears nowhere else in the paper before or after. The theorem statement is not self-contained.

## 3. Architecture — the question I was asked to spend the most time on

### 3.1 The advertised architecture

Figure 1 (p. 2) promises a route: **finite Stein normalization of Hitchin's cover** →
(with a marked bridge datum) → **chosen golden sign** → branching into **six ordered axes /
operator shadows** on one side and **ten labelled face axes / harmonic return** on the other.
Sections 5.2–5.3 and 4.3 are declared off-route.

### 3.2 What actually holds it together

The connective tissue between Theorem 1.1 and everything downstream is a **sign**, and the paper
is explicit, repeatedly, that the sign is not canonically transported:

- p. 3: "The datum also identifies the ten face axes with the two-subsets of those five labels."
- p. 4: "Here `Z_m` and `σ₃` are different cubics on spaces of dimensions five and four. The
  proposition compares their orientations relative to `m`; it does not identify their polynomial
  domains."
- p. 4: "The normalized component alone is not asserted to determine `m`."
- p. 12: "These relative source labels are attached to the normalized components, not to a
  pointwise regular-six-axis marking."
- p. 32 (Conclusion): "This compares orientation sources relative to the marking… and no map
  between their ambient harmonic representations is asserted."
- Appendix A, closing: "Proposition 1.2 makes no descent claim for them."

Every one of these disclaimers is correct and creditable. Together they establish that the
"source–shadow–return route" is not a theorem: it is a bookkeeping convention under which two
independently-computed objects are given compatible signs. Appendix A even tabulates the six
auxiliary choices and confirms that the comparison is invariant under exactly two of them
(switching, coordinated relabelling) and *not* under the other four.

So the honest description of the architecture is: **Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 6.1 are independent
results that the paper has arranged to have compatible signs.** Removing Theorem 1.1 would not
change Theorem 6.1's proof by a single line, and vice versa. The `√5` in Theorem 1.1 and the
`√5` in the icosahedral geometry are the same `√5` for the obvious reason (both come from the
icosahedron), not because one is transported into the other.

I want to be fair here: a paper *may* be a study of one object from several sides. Hitchin's own
two papers are like that. But this paper's framing device — the marking `m`, the "source–shadow–
return" scheme, Figure 1 — promises a derivation, and the text then spends a substantial fraction
of its words retracting the promise. The retraction is honest and the promise is not; the fix is
to drop the promise.

### 3.3 Result by result

**Earns its place on the main route:**

- **Theorem 1.1** (arithmetic incidence cover). This is the paper's real geometric contribution.
  The branch-cycle computation (`K_I = π*O(−4)`, `R ∼ 3h`, `π_*R = 6h`) is clean, short, and
  self-contained. The "square class from one fibre" lemma is well isolated and correctly used.
  The Stein-algebra argument (rank-one reflexive on `P⁶` is a line bundle; `v(a) ≥ 2` contradicts
  normality; hence `2d = 6`) is nicely done. The result is modest — it pins one rational constant
  in a square class Hitchin left unfixed — but it is genuine and it is proved.
- **§2.2** (rational incidence model). Necessary: without a `Q`-model there is no function field
  to compute.
- **§4.1** (golden fibre) and **§4.2** (the exchanger `R`). Prop. 4.1 supplies the complete fibre
  used in Theorem 1.1's proof; `R` is used in §3.2 to transport the conjugate configuration. Both
  load-bearing.
- **§3.1–3.2** (normalization over the Clebsch chart, golden involution). This is the actual
  content of Proposition 1.2 and it is correct and pretty: the factorization
  `z² = 80σ₃² = (z−4√5σ₃)(z+4√5σ₃)`, the split-pinching lemma, and the exchanger computation
  `dᵢdⱼC_{p(i)p(j)} = −C_{ij}` are all verified and all necessary.

**Earns its place, but not on the route the paper draws:**

- **Theorems 5.2, 5.3, 5.4** (recognition, rigidity, nonsingular minors). These are a genuinely
  coherent unit and the best mathematics in Section 5. Theorem 5.3's proof in particular is
  elegant: use `A₅`-equivariance of the Jacobian, the fact that every `A₅`-irreducible has an
  order-three fixed vector, to reduce a 20×15 rank computation to a 8×5 one that fits on the page.
  I verified both the reduced table and the full rank 14. Proposition 5.4's replacement of the
  cubic identity by nonvanishing of the twenty complementary 3×3 minors is a nice sharpening, and
  its proof (the `k ∈ {2,3}` ∩ `k ∈ {1,2}` squeeze) is clean.
  But note what they are *about*: they are about the order-six conference matrix as such. They do
  not use Hitchin's cover, the descent, or the marking. Theorem 5.3 is stated "at the golden
  representative" but the representative is just `C`; "golden" is decoration.

**Appendix in disguise:**

- **Theorem 5.5** (balanced exchange rigidity). Attractive, self-contained, correct — and the
  paper says outright that it "supplies no hypothesis" to anything else. Its stated motivation
  ("It explains why this six-axis carrier has no cut-independent higher-order analogue") is a
  motivation for a question nobody in this paper asked.
- **Theorem 5.6 + Remarks 5.7, 5.8** (aligned-design faithfulness). This is the strongest
  standalone result in the paper and it has *nothing* to do with the Clebsch cubic. Its inputs are
  the two-graph parity axiom, `R(3,3) = 6`, and a finite case analysis. Its context is
  reconstruction-up-to-complementation and principal-minor assignment. Remark 5.8's careful
  positioning against Holtz–Sturmfels, Rising–Kulesza–Taskar, Brunel, and the adaptive-vs-fixed
  distinction is the work of someone who knows that literature. All of it belongs in a
  combinatorics journal, alone, where it would be read by the people who care. Here it is 7 pages
  in the middle of an algebraic geometry paper and it will be skipped.
- **§4.3** (spinor specialization, `θ(R) = [2]`, nontrivial mod 11). Pure decoration. Nothing
  downstream uses it, and the paper itself defuses the only reason a reader might think it matters:
  "the coincidence of the two elevens is accidental." Correct, and correctly disclaimed — which is
  precisely the definition of a result present because it was available.
- **§2.1's quadratic-pinching discussion** (¾ of a page, plus a proof). Explicitly not used:
  "This local model explains the descent geometry but does not replace the comparison with
  Hitchin's incidence scheme below." It is good intuition; it is not an argument; it should be a
  remark of four lines.
- **The six-arc conclusion in Prop. 4.1**, and its characteristic-`p` persistence. Never used.

**The odd one out:**

- **Theorem 6.1** (degree-six harmonic restriction). This is a self-contained exact computation
  and a pretty one. Its dependence on the rest of the paper is exactly one bit: the sign of `y`.
  Its independent interest — an exact rational restriction of the classical bond-order observable
  `W₆` to a labelled `A₅` four-space, with the Gaunt factorization separating the universal
  angular-momentum denominator `11·13·17·19` from the geometric factor `1960/27` — is real, and it
  is the kind of thing a physics-adjacent audience would want. That audience will not find it here.

### 3.4 What the paper should be

Three papers:

1. **The arithmetic of Hitchin's incidence cover.** Theorem 1.1, §§2–4.2, §3.1–3.2, and Theorem
   6.1 as the closing section (its geometric content — that the marked Petersen realization returns
   an exact rational multiple of `σ₃` — is a legitimate coda). About 15 pages, and a much better
   read.
2. **Cubic recognition for conference matrices.** Theorems 5.1–5.4. About 8 pages.
3. **Reconstructing a two-graph from its aligned four-sets.** Theorem 5.5, Theorem 5.6,
   Remarks 5.7, 5.8. About 8 pages, and the one I would referee most enthusiastically.

## 4. Correctness and honesty of statement, theorem by theorem

### 4.1 Overall

Hypotheses are, with the exceptions below, stated where used and actually needed; conclusions do
not exceed proofs. The paper's characteristic move is to state a result and immediately bound it
("This does not strengthen the integral statement…", "no count of regular icosahedra is used to
exclude hidden boundary points", "we do not replace the finite exceptional set by `p ≠ 2,5`").
That habit is a real virtue and it should be preserved through the rewrite.

### 4.2 The one circular passage: the constant 16 (§2.1, pp. 5–6)

The paper says, in the same paragraph:

> "In the appendix Hitchin rescales the moment operator before the final restriction calculation;
> we therefore use the opening formula only to fix the hypersurface, not to identify the two
> printed scales."

and then

> "There is consequently a unique rational multiple, denoted `J₀`, for which Hitchin's appendix
> calculation [2, Appendix, final displayed formula] reads `ι*ₜJ₀ = 16σ₃²`."

and then

> "The coefficient is fixed by the chosen lift: at `y°`, the ratio of `J₀(xyz)` to `σ₃(y°)²` is
> exactly 16."

These cannot all be doing what they appear to. If `J` is *any* rational equation of the
hypersurface, then `ι*J = cσ₃²` for some `c ∈ Q×`, and `J₀ := (16/c)J` satisfies `ι*J₀ = 16σ₃²`
by construction. So the third sentence is a tautology, and the second presents as read-off-from-
Hitchin something the first sentence has just declined to read off.

This is not an error — the theorem is still correct — but the reader cannot see *why*. The actual
invariant, which the paper knows (p. 11: "The magnitude `4√5` is forced by the product of the
descent class 5 and the chart factor 16"), is the square class of the product: twist × chart scalar
`= 5·16 ≡ 5` in `Q×/Q×²`. Rescaling `J₀` moves the 5 and the 16 in compensating ways. **The paper
should state that invariant in §2.1 and derive both constants from it**, rather than fixing 16 by
fiat and then reporting 5 as a discovery. As written, a sceptical referee will suspect the 5 was
put in by hand, and it takes real work to see that it was not.

### 4.3 "Four exact descriptions" is an overclaim

Abstract, Figure 2, Theorem 5.1, and the Conclusion all present four descriptions of the same
cubic: triangle holonomy, middle-exterior diagonal, commutator Pfaffian, oriented cross-golden
determinant. In fact:

- `Z_T` is **defined** as `¼Σ(K_T)_{SS}x_S`, so "middle-exterior diagonal `= Z_T`" is the
  definition;
- the coefficient of `x_S` in `Pf[Dₓ,A]` is the signed complementary minor `det A[Sᶜ,S]` **for
  every symmetric `A` of order six** — the paper says so itself, on p. 16, immediately above the
  theorem: *"Only the triangle comparison is matrix-specific."* So the Pfaffian description is the
  same statement as the previous one, for any matrix at all;
- the cross-golden determinant is the block rewriting of `det[Dₓ,C] = 16Z²` under `C² = 5I`, with
  the sign supplied by an explicit convention: *"At `T₀`, orient these determinant lines so that
  the following equality has positive sign."* I confirmed the magnitude `10√5` and that the sign
  is genuinely a choice.

So there is **one** matrix-specific identity — `(K_T)_{SS} = 4T(S)`, i.e. triangle holonomy —
plus two general facts and a normalization. The body of §5.1 says this plainly and to the author's
credit. The abstract and conclusion do not, and a referee reading only those will feel misled when
they reach p. 16.

### 4.4 Theorem 5.2 is proved by less than its hypothesis

The hypothesis is `Φ_A = μT_A` with `μ ≠ 0`. The proof uses exactly two consequences: degrees match
(`n/2 = 3`) and `T_A` is translation-invariant (because `[D_{x+t1},A] = [Dₓ,A]`). Nothing else about
the Pfaffian enters. So the theorem is really:

> If `A` is real symmetric hollow with all off-diagonal entries nonzero, `n` even, `T_A` is
> translation-invariant, and `deg T_A = n/2`, then `n = 6` and `A² = λI`, `λ > 0`.

That is a fine theorem and the paper is not wrong to state it in the proportionality form. But the
abstract's "nonzero proportionality of the commutator Pfaffian to the triangle cubic already forces
order six and a scalar square" invites the reader to think the Pfaffian's structure is doing work,
and it is not. The paper partly owns this — "What the theorem leaves open is not the conclusion
`A² = λI` … but which weighted matrices among them satisfy the proportionality; those are not
classified" — which is the right disclaimer in the wrong place. Put it in the theorem's statement.

Minor: the abstract drops the "even order `n ≥ 4`" hypothesis.

### 4.5 Asserted-because-computed

Only one number in the paper cannot be checked from the page: `(1/4π)∫F_y³ = −15680000/1247103` at
`y = (4,−1,−1,−1,−1)` (§6.1). The paper describes the method (expand `P₆`, apply the double-
factorial monomial-moment formula) and defers the arithmetic to the bundles of Appendix B. I
reproduced it exactly in `Q[φ]`, so it is right; but a referee will have to run something, and the
paper should say so in the theorem's vicinity rather than only in Appendix B.

Everything else that is computational is displayed and hand-checkable: table (3), the two orbit

## 5. Claims about the literature

This is the strongest non-mathematical part of the paper, and it is phrased more defensibly than
most submissions I would see.

**Well phrased:**

- Novelty claims are scoped by named search domains rather than asserted flatly: "to our knowledge
  neither appears in the Pfaffian-commutator, compound-minor, determinantal-representation,
  conference-matrix, tight-frame, or maximal-determinant design literature" (p. 19). Naming the
  domains is exactly right.
- The paper separates its claim from the adjacent one it could be confused with, in detail. On
  Dammak–Lopez–Pouzet–Si Kaddour [25]: "their `v ≥ 7` is the endpoint of the admissible range
  `4 ≤ k ≤ v−3`, and the sharpness they remark on is in `k`, so no six-point failure follows from
  their work for either observable." That is the sentence a referee wants and rarely gets.
- Remark 5.8 concedes ground it did not have to: "The count above improves on none of that and is
  not offered as an algorithm." Likewise the Kummerfeld–Ramsey paragraph explicitly declines to
  compare the two quartet counts.
- §6: "It does not claim priority for the ambient icosahedral decomposition or for the bond-order
  invariant." Correct and necessary — the icosahedral decomposition of degree-six harmonics is
  classical [10,11].
- Theorem 5.2's classical ingredients are laid out before the novelty claim (Beauville, Tanturri,
  Huang–Oeding, the conference/two-graph/ETF correspondence), and the claim is narrowed to "the
  converse direction". Correctly narrowed.
- §2.3's integral boundary paragraph is a model of scoping a negative: it says exactly which
  geometric steps are missing (integral harmonic lattice, flatness, normality, Stein base change),
  and concludes "extra divisors of `N` are evidence gaps rather than additional bad primes
  predicted by the quadratic equation."

**Not defensible as phrased:**

- **"Hitchin supplies the incidence construction and every formula used here"** (p. 4). "Every
  formula" is unfalsifiable and, taken literally, is contradicted by the paper's own normalization
  work in §2.1 and by everything in §§3.2, 5, 6. Say which formulas.
- **"Dye's finite theorem gives the complementary square-5 criterion for Clebsch hexagons [4]"**
  (p. 4) and "Dye's existence criterion for Clebsch hexagons has the same [5] boundary [4, Thm 1]"
  (p. 14). Complementary to what? What criterion? Neither passage says what Dye proves, and Dye is
  never used. Either state the theorem or drop it.
- **The novelty claims rest on an audit the reader cannot see.** "We have not located [X] in the
  bounded audit documented with the distributed source archive" is honest about its own limits, but
  a referee cannot evaluate a search whose scope lives outside the manuscript. Given that Appendix
  B promises a summary and does not deliver one, the practical effect is that three novelty claims
  (Thm 5.2, Thm 5.5, Thm 5.6) are unverifiable from the paper. Move the OPER-3/OPER-4 scope rows
  into Appendix B verbatim; they are a few lines.

## 7. Summary judgement

The mathematics is right. I tested twenty-odd independent claims, including several — table (4),
the rank-14 Jacobian, the sharpness of `n = 7`, the exact value `−784000/1247103` — that a careless
author would have got wrong, and all of them are exactly as printed. The author's habit of bounding
each claim immediately after making it is a genuine strength and is rarer than it should be.

What the paper lacks is a thesis. It is a well-verified collection of five or six results about
objects that share the number five, presented as a derivation whose central step the text itself
declines to assert. The fix is structural, not mathematical: separate the combinatorics, drop the
decorative sections, state the arithmetic invariant that makes `5` and `16` two halves of one
square class, and rewrite the abstract so that somebody can tell from it what was proved.

**62/100; accept at 75; the gap is entirely architecture and exposition.**
