The Non-Attacking Queens Game how a university-level math problem became a sub-25-second exact solver

On Sunday evening, June 14, a university-level math problem escaped its intended audience. The programmer's son—in sixth grade, but studying university-level mathematics with a university math professor—had been pointed at the Non-Attacking Queens Game and told to “go learn about minimax and alpha–beta pruning.” His father sat down to help and got nerd-sniped.

The game is easy to state. Two players place queens on a chessboard, one at a time, without allowing any queen to attack another. The first player who has nowhere left to move loses. But one question hides an enormous search: on a 16×16 board, who wins if both players are perfect?

The father is a semi-retired programmer with two recent obsessions: agentic coding and low-level performance engineering. This was an unusually clean test of both. There were no users or deadlines, the correct result was known, and every speed claim could be measured.

His first complete version proved that the second player wins in about 56 minutes, after evaluating 10,017,867,872 nodes in the game tree. Nine days later, the current solver reached the same verdict after 308 million nodes in about 23.4 seconds of search: a 33-fold drop in node evaluations and a roughly 140-fold drop in wall time.

On Saturday night, with the solver near 52 seconds, the programmer gave Claude, the coding agent working in the repository, this brief:

“n=16 30s do not give up. do not stop for answers from me. be creative. do not get stuck or give up. work on a branch if you are worried about messing up main or create a new solver variant. this is a hobby project with no users so don't wimp out. i don't want to wake up in the morning and find you gave up. so please: be relentless”

Overnight goal, June 20–21

Claude spent the night reopening an old negative result. The discarded experiment had used a processor's bit-extraction instruction for a different task and much smaller cases. This time, the instruction made it cheaper to turn a board position into a compact graph. The solver could now decide every endgame with up to 16 available squares directly, instead of sending it through the main search. The next clean run finished in 33.9 seconds. 1What the game actually is

An empty chessboard offers room to roam. The first queen changes that at once, attacking every square along her row, column, and two diagonals.

The players take turns placing queens on squares that remain safe. Each new queen must avoid all the others, and each one erases more possible moves. Eventually no safe square remains. The player facing that board loses.

The rules produce no draws and leave no room for opinion. With perfect play, the first player can either force a win or cannot. On a 16×16 board, the second player wins; on an 18×18 board, the first player wins (Section 14). The difficulty lies in proving it.

The one picture to keep

At every turn, the board splits into available squares (empty, safe, and legal) and everything else. A move can only shrink the available set. The game is a controlled collapse, and the last legal placement wins.

Noon and Van Brummelen's game is the adversarial form of n-queens. Two players alternately place mutually non-attacking queens on an n×n board; under normal play, the player with no legal move loses. A queen at s blocks its row, column, and both diagonals.

A single blocked mask, occupied ∪ attacked, determines the position. Queens have no owner, and playing s always adds the same attack[s]; move history contributes nothing else. The complementary available set contains every legal move:

\( \text{available} = \text{board} \;\wedge\; \neg\,\text{blocked} \)

Every move strictly decreases |available|. The 16×16 board fits in Bits([u64; 4]), with square (r,c) at bit r·n+c. Self-inclusive attack[s] masks are precomputed once.

// geom.rs — a square attacks its row, col, and both diagonals (and itself)
if rr == r || cc == c
   || (rr as i32 - cc as i32) == (r as i32 - c as i32)   // ╲ diagonal
   || (rr + cc) == (r + c)                                // ╱ diagonal
{ mask.set(rr * n + cc); }

The production move order is dynamic. At each node, children are ranked by child0.popcount() ascending, with child0 == 0 first. Replacing the static empty-board degree order with this current-position score cut n=16 nodes 34.3% and wall time 30.2% against the preceding ETC configuration.

Where the game came from

The familiar n-queens puzzle asks for a completed arrangement. The game adds an adversary: if two people build the arrangement one queen at a time, who can force the last move?

The modern trail begins with Hassan Noon, who studied the game in a 2002 undergraduate thesis and later published the work with Glen Van Brummelen. They settled every odd board and the smaller even boards through 10. About a decade later, Thomas Jenrich found the problem on an open-problem list and attacked it independently with a small Pascal program. Only while preparing his paper did he learn of Noon's work.

Jenrich's 2014 computation reached the 16×16 board. It took almost 23 hours on a 1 GHz Pentium III and agreed that the second player wins. That result provides an unusually valuable check on this project: two unrelated programs, written years apart and built around different algorithms, reach the same full-scale verdict.

The game draws on several branches of mathematics and computer science. Combinatorial game theory supplies the language of winning and losing positions. Graph theory reveals when different-looking boards are really the same game. Exact-algorithm engineering decides whether the proof finishes in hours, minutes, or seconds.

G. Schrage described a restricted strategy version in 1989. The present rules trace to Hassan A. Noon's 2002 B.A. thesis, Surreal Numbers and the N-Queens Game, and the 2006 paper with Glen Van Brummelen. Their results cover all odd n and even boards through n=10. Jenrich began independently from Martin Erickson's open-problem list and learned of Noon's work while submitting his 2014 paper and QPGAME3 source.

The available-square conflict graph identifies the game as Node Kayles. That connects the recurrence to impartial combinatorial game theory and Sprague–Grundy values; generalized Node Kayles outcome determination is PSPACE-complete. For this board family, the engineering problem is exact exponential search under heavy transposition pressure. OEIS A344227 records full nimbers through n=13.

What Jenrich actually computed—and how it would parallelize today

Jenrich did not remember every board he had seen. He searched the same future again whenever a different move order reached it, but kept the program tiny and used symmetry plus a hand-built opening book for the second player's first two replies. The 16×16 proof made 71,461,975,237 recursive calls. Its 28 independent opening cases could be handed to separate modern cores with almost no communication. Even on the original machine, perfect root scheduling would reduce the 22h55m total to roughly the longest opening case—about 1h26m. A modern port would be much faster again, but quoting an exact time without compiling and benchmarking that code would be fiction.

QPGAME3 is a small backtracker using bit-pattern row/column/diagonal legality, D₄ restriction at the first move, conditional half-turn symmetry, move-order/“forbidden” pruning, and a hand-built book for player 2's first two replies at n=16. It has no TT or heap-resident search structure. The published n=16 run made 71.462 billion calls across 28 symmetry-distinct openings in 22h55m30s.

Those openings are independent. The largest contains 4.471 billion calls, so ideal root scheduling at the original call rate has a critical path of 4.471/71.462 = 6.26%, or about 1h26m. Load imbalance limits the gain to 16× rather than 28×. A modern many-core port should scale cleanly at that boundary because it shares neither a TT nor a bandwidth-heavy store. Splitting within an opening is harder because speculative siblings can weaken first-losing-child cutoffs. The algorithm would still evaluate 71 billion calls against the current solver's 396 million nodes, so a modern wall time requires a real port and benchmark.

2Why it is hard

A direct program starts with 256 possible first moves. Each opens dozens of replies, which open still more. Long before the board fills, the number of possible move sequences has become uselessly large.

Fortunately, many sequences lead to the same future. If one path chooses square A and then B, while another chooses B and then A, the remaining game may be identical. A solver can remember that answer once and reuse it.

That still leaves roughly 7.2 billion distinct 16×16 positions. At only 8 bytes each, they would occupy about 58 gigabytes, more than twice the memory available on the machine used here. From that point on, speed and memory became the same problem.

3Impartial games and who wins

Once a position is on the board, its history disappears. Queens have no owner, and both players see the same legal moves. Mathematicians call such a game impartial.

For an impartial game where the last player to move wins, every board is one of two types:

  • A losing board for whoever has to move (a P-position). Whatever you do, you hand your opponent a winning board.
  • A winning board (an N-position): at least one of your moves hands the opponent a losing board.

This gives the solver its entire logic: a position is winning if at least one move hands the opponent a losing position. If every move hands over a win, the position is losing. Repeating that rule backward from finished boards eventually classifies the empty 16×16 board as a loss for the first player.

A free shortcut for odd boards

Odd boards yield to a short argument. The first player takes the center, then mirrors every opposing move through that center. The mirror square is always legal, so the first player always has a reply and must make the last move. Even boards have no center square. That missing square is why 16×16 needs a computation at all.

The game is impartial (moves depend only on the position, not the player) and normal-play (last to move wins). The recursive win/loss relation is the negamax characterization:

\( \text{wins}(P) \;=\; \exists\, \text{move } m:\; \neg\,\text{wins}(P \text{ after } m) \)

A position with no moves is a loss for the player to move. Everything else folds from there. The empty 16×16 board evaluates to a loss for the mover ⇒ second player wins, matching Jenrich (arXiv:1312.5135) and OEIS A344227 (our verdicts agree through n=13; the win/loss verdict holds through 16).

The odd-n O(1) theorem

Odd boards are decided without search by a pairing strategy: the first player takes the center, then answers each reply with its 180° rotation. The mirror move is always legal because a square attacks its own 180° image only on a center line, so off-center the pairing survives to the end. The solver short-circuits this:

fn first_player_wins(&self, q) -> bool {
    if q.is_odd() { return true; }   // centre + 180° mirror, no search
    /* … the real search for even n … */
}
The formal version — P-positions, N-positions, and why the recursion is sound

For an impartial normal-play game, define the set of P-positions (previous-player-wins, i.e. the mover loses) as the unique set \(\mathcal P\) satisfying: (i) every terminal position is in \(\mathcal P\); (ii) from every position in \(\mathcal P\), every move leads outside \(\mathcal P\); (iii) from every position not in \(\mathcal P\), some move leads into \(\mathcal P\). Existence and uniqueness follow by induction on the (finite, acyclic-by-monotone-popcount) game DAG.

The win/loss bit is exactly \( \mathbb 1[P \notin \mathcal P] \). Because the available popcount strictly decreases on every move, the game graph is a DAG with bounded depth \(\le n^2\), so the recursion terminates and memoization is well-defined. Soundness of transposition merging follows from the impartial, history-free structure: the value of a node depends only on its blocked mask, never on the path taken to reach it — so there is no graph-history-interaction (GHI) hazard, and identical masks may share a stored value.

4Symmetry — the first big way to visit fewer positions

The first large reduction comes from the square itself. Rotate a position by 90 degrees, or reflect it across an axis, and its outcome cannot change. These eight orientations form D₄.

Before storing a position, the solver generates all eight views and chooses one as the official version. Any rotated or reflected copy lands on the same entry. A generic position therefore needs one record instead of eight.

The square's dihedral group D₄ has eight elements: identity, three rotations, and four reflections. Acting on the available mask partitions positions into orbits with identical game values. The TT stores one canonical representative per orbit, chosen as the lexicographically smallest transformed mask.

fn canon(&self, mask) -> Bits {            // geom.rs
    let mut best = mask;
    for t in 1..8 {                        // the 7 non-identity symmetries
        let img = permute(mask, &self.sym[t]);
        if img < best { best = img; }       // lexicographic min over the orbit
    }
    best
}
fn pos_key(&self, blocked) -> Bits {
    self.canon(self.board.and_not(blocked)) // canonicalise AVAILABLE, not blocked
}

Canonicalizing available rather than blocked preserves the same equivalence classes because one is a pure function of the other. Deep in the tree, however, available is sparse, so each permutation moves fewer set bits. That representation change cut n=14 from 79.6s to 33.0s with an identical node set.

Intuition · group actions and orbits

The eight transforms partition positions into orbits. Taking the minimum image assigns every member of an orbit the same deterministic key, so lookup operates on the quotient space without storing orbit metadata.

The formal version — D₄ orbits and Burnside counting

\(D_4 = \langle r, s \mid r^4 = s^2 = 1,\; srs = r^{-1}\rangle\), order 8. It acts on the set of board states; the orbit of a state \(x\) is \(\mathcal O(x) = \{g\cdot x : g \in D_4\}\), and by orbit–stabilizer \(|\mathcal O(x)| = |D_4| / |\mathrm{Stab}(x)| \in \{1,2,4,8\}\) (most positions have trivial stabilizer ⇒ orbit size 8). The number of distinct positions up to symmetry is the orbit count, given by Burnside's lemma:

\( \displaystyle \#\text{orbits} \;=\; \frac{1}{|D_4|}\sum_{g \in D_4} |\mathrm{Fix}(g)| \)

where \(\mathrm{Fix}(g)\) is the set of positions invariant under \(g\). Most positions are fixed only by the identity, so the average is close to \(\tfrac18\) of the raw count — hence the ~8× reduction. The canonical-representative trick computes the same quotient constructively: \(\mathrm{key}(x) = \min_{g}\, g\cdot x\) under the lexicographic order on the bitset, which is constant on orbits by construction.

Roots: how symmetry shrinks the very first move

The same reduction applies to the opening move. A naive solver starts 256 searches, one from each square. Folding rotations and reflections together leaves only the openings in one triangular wedge of the board. These distinct starting cases are the search roots.

D₄ acts on the opening square too. Canonicalising a first move to its orbit representative (the lexicographically-minimal of its 8 images) collapses ~8 openings into one, so the solver only launches a search from each distinct orbit. The number of roots is the number of D₄ orbits of board squares — about ⌈n²/8⌉, lifted slightly because squares on a symmetry axis (the diagonal or the mid-lines) have smaller orbits (size 4, or 1 at the centre), not the full 8.

5The available set as a graph — merging far more

Board symmetry recognizes sameness that the eye can see. Graph theory finds sameness that the board conceals.

Ignore the coordinates and draw one dot for every available square. Join two dots if queens on those squares would attack each other. The resulting network contains everything that matters about future play. Two boards with the same network are the same game, even if no rotation or reflection connects them.

This observation merged roughly 3.4 times as many positions as board symmetry alone. It also introduced a new cost: comparing graph shapes can take longer than the memory it saves. Later solvers kept the mathematical insight while removing the expensive comparison from the live search.

The available graph has one vertex per legal square and an edge between mutually attacking squares. The game is Node Kayles on this graph, so graph isomorphism preserves value and forms a coarser equivalence than D₄. Measured graph/D₄ merge ratios are 2.63, 3.17, 3.42, and 3.40× at n=8, 10, 12, and 14. Applied to n=16, the observed plateau suggests about 2.1 billion graph-distinct states instead of 7.2 billion D₄-distinct states.

Canonicalization can erase that gain. The full Weisfeiler–Leman path costs roughly 4,200 cycles per key against about 62 for carried D₄, so a pure graph-keyed search loses despite fewer states. iso-flat used graph keys only where their merge paid for the key, with graph and D₄ values in disjoint namespaces:

fn node_key(&self, q, orient) -> Bits {        // iso_flat.rs
    let pc = avail.popcount();
    if pc <= self.iso_max_avail {              // small graph: exact iso, cheap
        let h = if pc <= 7 { iso_key_tiny_table(avail) }  // complete ≤7 lookup
                else        { iso_key_fast(avail) };       // WL canon
        graph_bits(h)                          // tagged graph namespace
    } else {
        d4_bits(lex_min8(orient))              // large graph: cheap D4, disjoint tag
    }
}

For ≤7-vertex graphs the value is resolved by a complete tiny table keyed by the raw labelled edge code (a 28-bit upper-triangular bitstring) — no canonicalization at all, just one direct byte load, because the Node-Kayles value is relabelling-invariant. The default threshold is 7 (the top of the no-WL regime). The two namespaces (graph_bits / d4_bits) never collide even on a full 16×16 board where no spare board bit exists.

Lineage note: the selective-key code above explains iso-flat and why graph equivalence mattered. Today's K=17 iso-dense bypasses it: pc≤17 is evaluated by WK with no canonical key or TT entry, while pc≥18 uses the carried D₄ representative (and pc=18 itself skips even that). The current solver keeps the graph-game model but not the live WL canonicalizer.

Intuition · graph isomorphism and color refinement

Two graphs are "the same shape" if you can rename the dots of one to turn it into the other. Checking that directly can require trying many renamings. Color refinement narrows the possibilities: paint every dot by its degree, then repeatedly repaint it from the multiset of its neighbours' colors. Structurally different dots often separate quickly. But refinement alone is only a filter—some non-isomorphic regular graphs remain indistinguishable. The exact tiny-graph table used by the default live path avoids that ambiguity; larger historical experiments added individualization and were checked against an exact canonicalizer.

The formal version — 1-WL color refinement

Seed each vertex with its degree: \(c_0(v) = \deg(v)\). Iterate

\( c_{i+1}(v) \;=\; \mathrm{hash}\!\Big(c_i(v),\; \{\!\{\, c_i(u) : u \in N(v) \,\}\!\}\Big) \)

where \(\{\!\{\cdot\}\!\}\) is a multiset, so neighbour order is irrelevant. The partition refines monotonically and stabilizes in \(\le |V|\) rounds. Isomorphic graphs always produce the same stable-color invariant, but the converse is false: 1-WL can collide on non-isomorphic regular graphs. The project therefore used a complete precomputed canonical table for the default pc≤7 live key. Its larger-graph iso_key_fast added individualization signatures and matched the exact individualization-refinement canonicalizer on the tested n≤14 corpus, but was slower and is absent from current iso-dense. The full canonicalizer branches on ambiguous color classes and selects the lexicographically minimum adjacency certificate.

6Sprague–Grundy: splitting a game into independent pieces

Near the end, the surviving squares sometimes break into clumps that can no longer affect one another. A move in one clump leaves every other clump untouched. The board has become several smaller games played in parallel.

The Sprague–Grundy theorem assigns each independent game a number, called a nimber. The nimbers combine by XOR, and a total of zero marks a loss for the player to move.

Queens attack across long rows and diagonals, so the board rarely splits until late in the game. This elegant theory never became a major speedup here. That failure established a recurring rule: reducing the number of positions helps only when the shortcut costs less than the positions it removes.

When the available-graph splits into disconnected components, the position is a disjunctive sum of independent impartial games. By the Sprague–Grundy theorem each component has a nimber (Grundy value), and the position's nimber is their XOR (nim-sum); the position is a loss for the mover iff the nimber is 0.

\( \mathcal G(P) \;=\; \bigoplus_{c \in \text{components}} \mathcal G(c), \qquad \text{mover loses} \iff \mathcal G(P) = 0 \)

Each component's nimber is the mex (minimum excludant) of its children's nimbers:

\( \mathcal G(c) \;=\; \operatorname{mex}\{\, \mathcal G(c') : c' \text{ a child of } c \,\} \)

// nimber.rs — full Sprague-Grundy, no α-β cutoff (mex needs every child)
let mut seen = 0u64;                       // bitset of child nimbers (< n ≤ 16)
for &sq in &q.order {
    if q.is_available(blocked, sq) {
        seen |= 1u64 << self.grundy(q, q.place(blocked, sq));
    }
}
let mex = (!seen).trailing_zeros() as u8;  // smallest value not present

Two practical caveats specific to this game: (1) computing full nimbers has no α-β cutoff (mex needs the entire child set), so it costs ~265× the win/loss node count and is feasible only to ~n=12. (2) The queen graph is biconnected with long-range diagonals, so it does not decompose at the full-board level. A component-nimber oracle was built and cut nodes sharply, but its mex and decomposition cost made wall time up to 6.6× worse, so the live solver dropped it. The later W8 table is a separate boolean win/loss recurrence over all small labelled graphs; it does not compute or combine nimbers.

Intuition · mex and XOR

mex ("minimum excludant") is just: look at the set of values your moves can reach, and name the smallest non-negative integer that's missing. That missing value is this position's nimber — it measures "how much maneuvering room you have." A nimber of 0 means every move hands the opponent a non-zero (winning-for-them) position — you're stuck.

Why XOR? Imagine each game as a stack of coins in binary. Playing optimally across several games is exactly the classic game of Nim, and the winning condition for Nim is "make the XOR of the pile sizes zero." Nimbers turn any impartial game into an equivalent Nim pile, so combining games is combining Nim piles — and Nim adds with XOR. A zero nim-sum is a balanced, lost-for-the-mover position; any non-zero sum, you can always rebalance it to zero and win.

The formal version — the Sprague–Grundy theorem

Theorem (Sprague 1935, Grundy 1939). Every impartial game under normal play is equivalent to a single Nim heap of size equal to its Grundy value \(\mathcal G\), where \(\mathcal G(P) = \operatorname{mex}\{\mathcal G(P') : P \to P'\}\) and \(\operatorname{mex}(S) = \min(\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\setminus S)\). For a disjunctive sum \(P = P_1 + \cdots + P_k\) (a move is a move in exactly one component), \(\mathcal G(P) = \mathcal G(P_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathcal G(P_k)\), where \(\oplus\) is bitwise XOR. The position is a P-position (mover loses) iff \(\mathcal G(P) = 0\). The proof of the XOR rule is the Nim strategy argument: from \(\mathcal G(P)\neq 0\) there is a move zeroing the nim-sum (clear the leading bit), and from \(\mathcal G(P)=0\) every move makes it non-zero — exactly the P/N alternation. Verified against OEIS A344227 through n=12.

The solver's history is a succession of bottlenecks. The first versions repeated too much work. The next remembered so much that they ran out of memory. Compression made the search fit but made lookups slow. A flat table made lookups cheap, after which the endgames themselves became the problem.

Each generation answered the question raised by the previous one. Just as important, it often threw the previous machinery away.

The Solver implementations preserve the experimental lineage. D₄ memoization exposed the capacity wall. Graph-isomorphic keys reduced the set enough to fit but raised key cost. A flat lockless TT removed live archive overhead. Exact W layers removed the dense tail, dynamic ordering improved cutoffs, and BMI2 row extraction moved the profitable WK ceiling from 12 to 16. The reversal that mattered

By Thursday, the solver appeared to have settled at about 36 million positions per second. A full run should take roughly 3m41s, and the notes called that “the wall.” The number was precise, repeatable, and wrong.

The machine was carrying stale data in compressed swap and disk caches, leaving too little room for the solver's table. Many tests had measured eviction rather than the intended algorithm. After the memory was cleared, the dense endgame table restored, and the main table forced onto large pages, the same problem finished in 2m44s. The episode changed the burden of proof: a measured floor had to agree with a first-principles estimate of the machine.

A "~36 M/s floor, sub-50 unreachable, n=16 ~3m41s wall" was recorded on a memory-degraded box: zram was full, ZFS ARC held ~13 GB, the 17 GB TT was capped, W8 had been tested through a slow path, and huge-page promotion was incomplete. A clean box plus the W8 table and MADV_COLLAPSE reached 2m44s. The first-principles estimate was already far below 3m41s; that gap called for an audit, not acceptance of the measured wall.

8Three ways the solver got fasterThree orthogonal axes of optimization

Every successful change altered one of three quantities: how many positions the solver visits, how many bytes it spends remembering each one, or how long it waits to reach a stored answer. Gains in one category multiply gains in the others.

  1. Visit fewer positions. Remember what you've solved; merge look-alikes (symmetry, graph shape); resolve tiny endgames from a lookup table instead of searching them.
  2. Store each position more cheaply. Don't store the whole board; store a tiny "fingerprint" of it. Pack everything into 8 bytes. Carry a compact form down the search so you rarely touch the full board.
  3. Reach each stored position faster. Each lookup is a random jump into a multi-gigabyte table. For much of the project, the processor spent more time waiting for those answers than computing them.

The 99× wall-time reduction decomposes into three levers. They multiply: a cheaper node matters more after ordering removes 90% of the nodes, and a better representation changes both capacity and probe latency. Most isolated hot-loop edits measured 0–5%; §11 retains the exceptions.

  1. Visit fewer nodes — memoization, D₄ canon, graph-iso canon, the complete W8 table, and the table-free W9–W17 evaluator hierarchy (never probe or normally expand pc≤17). (§3–8.)
  2. Represent nodes cheaper — 256-bit board as [u64;4]; the 8-byte TT slot with a 55-bit fingerprint; the in-band TinyGraph carried down so deep nodes never touch the 256-bit board. (§9.)
  3. Search them in a better order. Dynamic current-board ranking makes cutoffs arrive earlier; fused ETC checks the most promising cached children before expansion. This ordering win now matters more than another per-node micro-optimization. (§10.)

The W8 table — the lever that broke the wall

The first exact endgame table began at eight available squares. There are few enough labelled conflict graphs of that size to compute every answer at startup and pack them into 32 megabytes. Whenever the main search reaches eight squares, one lookup replaces the entire remaining subtree.

The table never forgets. The earlier cache could evict a small endgame and later search it again thousands of times; W8 always returns the answer. It cut the work by about 28%.

At pc == 8, the available graph has a 28-bit labelled edge code. That code directly indexes a complete, eviction-free 32 MiB bitset built bottom-up for W0..W8. The lookup needs no canonicalization, fingerprint, or normal TT probe.

Completeness is the performance property. iso-flat could evict pc=8 values from its 13–17 GB TT and later re-expand their ≤7 subtrees. W8 cannot miss logically or be evicted. Replacing that repeated work cut nodes from 7.1 billion to 5.1 billion, about 28%, while occupying 16 huge pages.

From W9 to W16 — compute the entry instead of storing it

Nine squares seem to demand the same treatment, but a complete W9 table would consume about 8 gigabytes. W10 would be incomparably larger. The solver instead computes only the entry it needs.

From a nine-square position, every move leaves at most eight squares. W8 already knows all of those children. The solver tries the possible moves on the compact graph and reads each child from W8, stopping as soon as it finds a losing reply for the opponent. No W9 table and no ordinary search are needed.

The same recurrence builds W10 from lower layers, then W11 and W12. For a time, W13 was slower because constructing its graph cost more than the subtree it removed. One cheaper bit-extraction operation changed that balance. W13 through W16 became profitable together, bringing the full solve to 33.9 seconds and 396 million nodes.

One layer further, to seventeen squares, paid as well—provided the evaluator also tried each move in the most-forcing order first, so a winning position bails at its first losing reply. That brought the search to roughly 24.5 seconds. Seventeen turned out to be the sweet spot: deeper layers do remove more positions, but each one costs proportionally more to evaluate, so the wall stops moving. The final step was the opposite of computing more—it was remembering less. For one specific endgame size (eighteen available squares), the solver now skips the entire ritual of canonicalizing and recording the position, because that size is uniquely safe to forget: every move from it lands in a square count the W hierarchy already answers directly. That saved the last bit of bookkeeping and brought the search to about 23.4 seconds and 308 million nodes.

Compute the entry, not the table

A complete 9-square table is too big to hold, but one requested entry can be derived from W8; that value can serve the next layer. The hierarchy trades arithmetic for avoided memory probes and entire subtrees. Cheaper graph construction moved the economic limit from 12 to 16, and most-forcing-first evaluation carried it to 17. Beyond that the trade reverses: deeper layers conserve work rather than save it, because each removed position costs about as much to evaluate as the small subtree it stood in for.

iso-dense extends the recurrence without materializing higher tables. A complete labelled W9 has 2^(9·8/2) = 2³⁶ rows, or 8 GiB as a bitset. Instead, get9 computes one requested value from W0..W8. W10 through W16 recurse over the same lower evaluators. W9 shows the kernel:

9Representing positions cheaply

The solver now knew which positions it wanted to remember. It still needed to fit enough of them in memory. A full 256-square board was far too large to store billions of times, so the table kept only a compact identity tag.

Store a fingerprint, not the board. Instead of writing down the full board, the solver records a short 55-bit "fingerprint" of it—like a hash of a document. If two boards land in the same memory slot and have the same fingerprint, they're treated as equal. Could two genuinely different boards accidentally share a fingerprint? Yes, but the odds are about 1 in 36 quadrillion per colliding lookup, vanishingly small even across billions of probes. The independently known n=16 verdict agrees, adding confidence without proving that every internal lookup was collision-free. Each remembered entry is just 8 bytes—five times more entries per gigabyte than storing the board.

Carry a tiny form down the search. Once the game is down to a handful of squares, the solver stops touching the big 256-bit board at all and carries a tiny compact description instead — turning big board operations into single-byte operations.

The board occupies four u64 words. Storing all 32 bytes in every TT entry would make capacity impossible, so each slot keeps only {used:1, val:8, fp:55} in one u64.

// tt.rs — the entire slot is one u64
fn pack(fp, val) -> Slot {
    Slot(1 | ((val as u64) << 1) | ((fp & FP_MASK) << 9))
}   // bit 0 = used, bits 1..8 = value, bits 9..63 = 55-bit fingerprint

The slot index consumes about 31 routing bits. The fingerprint comes from an independent 64-bit hash half, so a foreign key already colliding on the slot matches with probability 2⁻⁵⁵. The 40-to-8-byte reduction provides five times as many slots per GiB. A fingerprint mismatch is a safe miss; a false match is possible but has vanishing probability. Jenrich's agreement checks the final verdict independently but does not prove that every internal probe was collision-free.

The TinyGraph ([u8;8]×2, which fits within one cache line) is built once when a node drops to popcount ≤7 and carried down so the deep tail does single-byte alive & !closed[i] operations instead of 256-bit board ops. Hot structs use plain data, contiguous storage, explicit #[repr], and compile-time size_of assertions so a field addition cannot silently change padding.

Intuition · why a 55-bit fingerprint is safe

It feels reckless to throw away the board and keep a hash. The intuition is the birthday paradox in reverse: a collision needs two keys to land in the same slot AND share all 55 fingerprint bits. The slot already separates keys by their routing bits, so within one slot you're asking 55 coin-flips to all match by chance—\(2^{-55}\), about one in 36 quadrillion. Even a Jenrich-scale 10¹¹ probes expects far less than one wrong hit, and a wrong hit would still have to alter the final verdict. Jenrich's independent agreement adds confidence, but does not by itself certify every internal lookup.

The formal version — fingerprint collision probability

A wrong hit requires a foreign key \(k'\neq k\) to (i) route to the same slot and (ii) match the stored 55-bit fingerprint. Conditioned on a colliding probe (a slot already occupied by a different key), the fingerprint — an independent hash half — matches with probability \(p = 2^{-55}\). Over \(N\) such colliding probes the expected number of wrong hits is \(N\cdot 2^{-55}\); for \(N \sim 10^{11}\) (Jenrich scale) that is \(\approx 2.8\times10^{-6}\). Even unconditionally, the probability of any wrong hit is bounded by \(1 - (1-2^{-55})^{N} \approx N\cdot 2^{-55} \ll 1\). The error is one-sided in the sense that a fingerprint mismatch is always a correct miss; only a spurious match can mislead, and the global verdict is independently cross-checked against Jenrich's n=16 = second-player result.

Rust and the Tiger-style hot-path rules

The solvers are written in Rust. The project calls its inner-loop contract “Tiger-style” discipline: keep frequently used data small and contiguous, move counters and tuning metadata aside, resolve settings before the search begins, and allow no allocation, locks, shared counters, environment reads, or system calls inside the per-position loop.

The implementation is Rust. “Tiger-style” is project shorthand for a compile-time-enforced hot-path contract:

  • Hot structs contain fixed-width plain data; dynamic containers and measurement state live outside them.
  • Storage is contiguous. Inner loops use indices and slices rather than linked objects or repeated pointer chasing.
  • Layout is explicit with #[repr(C)] or #[repr(transparent)]; compile-time size and alignment assertions catch accidental padding.
  • Fields are ordered by alignment and access temperature. Cold or optional fields move to sidecars.
  • Integer widths match their range: squares and small graph values use u8/u32, widening only at true indexing boundaries.
  • Run-constant modes are selected once, preferably through const-generic monomorphization. Production loops contain no dead instrumentation branch.
  • No per-node allocation, locks, atomics, environment lookup, syscall, or unreviewed raw-pointer design. The few unsafe blocks wrap huge-page allocation and prefetch and state their invariants.

These rules are not individually claimed as speedups. They prevent layout drift and coordination work from entering code that runs hundreds of millions of times.

10The bottleneck keeps movingThe moving bottleneck: memory, frontend, evaluator

For much of the week, each search step ended with a random jump into a 17-gigabyte table. The arithmetic was cheap; waiting for memory was not. Several optimizations therefore changed how the machine reached the table rather than how it reasoned about the game.

W16 and then W17 have changed the balance again. The current run stores so few large positions that the table is mostly empty, while exact endgame evaluation consumes more of the work. A fresh, careful profile of this new solver—not a repeat of the old "memory-bound" claim—found something unusual: the machine is busy almost the entire run yet maxes out nothing in particular. It even has spare capacity to fetch from memory that the single-threaded walk through the hard opening simply cannot use. That last fact quietly explains why every attempt to "look ahead" for the next memory read had failed: there was already idle fetch capacity, and no serial way to fill it.

  • One shared table, no locks. Many cores share one table with no "do not disturb" locks — safe because the same board always computes the same answer, so even if two cores write the same slot they write the same thing.
  • Look ahead. Before the solver needs a slot, it tells the processor "fetch this soon" (a prefetch), so the slow trip from RAM overlaps with other work instead of stalling.
  • Big memory pages. The processor keeps a small directory of where memory lives. A huge random-access table blows out that directory constantly. Forcing the memory into giant 2-megabyte pages shrinks the directory's job and cuts the stalls.
  • Count locally. Bumping a shared "positions searched" counter on every node would force the cores to constantly synchronize — so each core counts privately and reports in about once a second.

Before W12, random probes into a 17 GB TT exposed about 120 ns of DRAM latency. On the later K12 workload, TMA attributed roughly 30% of slots to backend memory and 31% to the frontend. W16/W17 then cut the run to ~308 million nodes and a few GB of live TT contents, while raising cycles per node through nested evaluation. The K17 saturation audit (Zen5 dispatch-stall counters) found the search bound by no single resource: IPC ≈ 1.4 against a peak near 6, and load- and store-queue-full both ~1%—the load queue's large headroom is unused MLP the serial DFS probe cannot fill. The low IPC is a distributed stall: frontend (i-cache, getK code) ~28% plus backend ~25%, not one wall. This is also why prefetch keeps measuring dead: the bottleneck was never a shortage of in-flight memory requests.

Lockless atomic transposition table

One flat Box<[AtomicU64]>, relaxed load/store, no mutex, no sharding. Safe by construction: a position's value is deterministic, so a concurrent write for the same key stores the same value, and a write for a different key is rejected by the fingerprint. This removes a lock/unlock and a mutex cache-line bounce from every node. (Hyatt's XOR-key trick is unnecessary — the 55-bit fingerprint self-validates identity.)

Lemire fastrange indexing

Map a 64-bit hash uniformly into [0, len) with one widening multiply + shift — no power-of-two table required, so the table can be sized to fill all available RAM:

\( \texttt{index}(h) \;=\; \big\lfloor (h \cdot \texttt{len}) / 2^{64} \big\rfloor \;=\; \texttt{(((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64)} \)

fn index(&self, route: u64) -> usize {
    ((route as u128).wrapping_mul(self.len as u128) >> 64) as usize
}

Hash-carry + software prefetch

Each key is hashed once at creation (hash128 → two independent halves (route, fp)); the halves are reused for the prefetch, the get, and the put. The solver issues _mm_prefetch on a child's slot before recursing, so the random-probe DRAM round-trip overlaps with the work in between.

Huge pages

A multi-GB table probed at random thrashes the dTLB. MADV_HUGEPAGE reached only about 73% 2 MiB coverage on a randomly faulted 17 GB table. Prefaulting with MADV_POPULATE_WRITE and forcing MADV_COLLAPSE reached 100%, cutting wall time about 5% at nearly identical node count. Huge-page collapse is default for tables of at least 4 GB.

Thread-local counters + const-generic monomorphisation

Node and HLL counters accumulate per thread and flush about once a second. A shared per-node fetch_add bounced between the processor's two CCXs and measured a 2× drag. Run-constant choices use const generics selected at the call site, leaving no per-node mode branch in the frontend-sensitive kernel.

Intuition · the TLB and huge-page story

Think of RAM as a vast warehouse and the processor as a clerk with a small index card listing where the last few hundred shelves are. Normally that index covers tiny 4 KB shelves. When the clerk is fetching random items from a 17 GB warehouse, the shelf he wants is almost never on his card — so every fetch is two trips: one to look up the location, one to grab the item. Huge pages make each shelf 500× bigger (2 MB), so one index card now covers 500× more warehouse and the location is usually already on the card. Same items, far fewer double-trips.

The standout · branchless move filter

At every position the solver walks its parent's move list and keeps the moves that are still legal. "Still legal?" is a near coin-flip deep in the tree, and a coin-flip is the worst thing you can ask a processor to predict — it guesses, misses about half the time, and stalls unwinding the wrong guess. The fix removes the question. Each candidate move is written down unconditionally, and the running count is nudged forward by a 0-or-1 — no "if." Same output, no gamble. Branch mispredictions fell about 34%, instructions-per-step about 9%, for roughly a 10% speedup.

Compacting the parent's move list to those still set in the child's avail was an if avail_has(sq) { buf[nc] = sq; nc += 1; } — a ~50/50 data-dependent branch the predictor misses every other node. Rewritten branchless: write the square unconditionally, then nc += avail_has8(avail, sq) as usize — an unavailable square is simply overwritten next iteration. The output slice is byte-identical (so the searched node set is unchanged), trading the misprediction for one always-taken L1 store. Measured ~34% fewer branch-misses, ~9% lower CPI, ~10% faster — the largest single per-node win in the kernel.

8-byte fingerprint slot

Don't store the board — store a 55-bit fingerprint of it, the way a hash stands in for a document. A wrong match needs a different board to land in the same slot and match all 55 bits: about one chance in 36 quadrillion, and the final answer is cross-checked anyway. Each entry shrinks to 8 bytes.

Each TT slot is one u64 packing {used:1, val:8, fp:55}. The slot index already pins the routing bits; the 55-bit fingerprint comes from an independent hash half, so a foreign same-slot hit matches with probability ≈2⁻⁵⁵. Slot 40 B → 8 B = 5× more entries per byte, with the available-mask key intact so every transposition still merges.

Hash-carry

A position gets hashed once, when it's created, and that hash is reused for everything done to it: the look-ahead fetch, the lookup, and the later store. The old code re-hashed the same board three times.

hash128(key) yields two independent halves (route, fp) once per child at creation; the halves are threaded to the prefetch, the get_h, and the put_h instead of re-deriving them at each touch. Folds three hashes of a 256-bit key into one on the hot recursive path.

Software prefetch

Before the solver actually needs a table slot, it tells the processor "go fetch this now." The slow trip out to RAM then happens in the background, overlapping with other work, instead of freezing the processor when the data is finally needed.

Each child's slot is _mm_prefetch'd (hint T0) right after its key is hashed, before the recursive call. Because the search is random-probe-latency-bound (~120 ns/probe), issuing the prefetch ahead of the demand get overlaps the DRAM round-trip with the intervening work — memory-level parallelism on otherwise serial stalls.

Lockless atomic table

Every core shares one table with no locks at all. It's safe because a board's answer never changes: if two cores compute and store the same board, they store the same value, and a different board is caught by its fingerprint. No lock means no waiting in line.

One flat Box<[AtomicU64]>, relaxed load/store, no mutex, no sharding. Sound by construction: a position's value is deterministic, so a concurrent same-key write stores the same bits, and a different key is rejected by the fingerprint. Removes a lock/unlock and a mutex cache-line bounce from every node. (No Hyatt XOR-key needed — the fingerprint self-validates identity.)

Thread-local counters

Bumping a shared "positions searched" tally on every step would force the cores to keep handing one number back and forth. Instead each core counts on its own and reports in roughly once a second.

The per-node node/HLL counter increments a thread-local integer, flushed to the shared atomic only every ~2¹⁸ nodes (≈1 s). A shared fetch_add per node bounces the cache line across the two core complexes over Infinity Fabric — measured a ~2× throughput drag. The shared totals are made exact again by a drain at search end.

Huge pages

The processor keeps a tiny directory of where recently-used memory lives. A random-access table many gigabytes wide blows that directory out constantly. Forcing the table onto 2-megabyte pages makes each directory entry cover 500× more memory, so the lookups stop double-tripping.

A random-probed multi-GB table thrashes the dTLB. MADV_HUGEPAGE alone only reaches ~73% 2 MB coverage on a randomly-faulted 17 GB table; MADV_POPULATE_WRITE + MADV_COLLAPSE forces 100% — a clean ~5% wall win with near-identical node counts (a genuine per-node TLB win). Default-on for tables ≥4 GB.

Compile-time flag folding

Some switches are fixed for an entire run — which key scheme, whether a measurement counter is on. Rather than re-checking them at every step, they're baked into the code as constants when it's compiled, so the inner loop never even sees the question.

Run-constant toggles (measurement mode, window flag, key mode) are resolved once into const generics at the subtree-handoff site, so the hot loop has no per-node branch on them — dead arms are compiled away. The single runtime decision picks the monomorphised instantiation once. This matters because the kernel is frontend/L1i-bound: a dead per-iteration branch would feed the predictor garbage and worsen the actual bottleneck.

Multiply-shift indexing

Turning a hash into a table slot would normally use a remainder (division), which is slow and forces the table to be a power-of-two size. One widening multiply plus a shift does the same job and frees the table to be any size — so it can be sized to fill all the RAM available.

Lemire's fastrange: (((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64) maps a 64-bit hash uniformly into [0, len) with one widening multiply and a shift — no modulo, no power-of-two constraint. The table can be sized to any slot count, straddling the 17 GB→34 GB power-of-two gap on a 26 GB box. The extra multiply is free against the random-probe latency.

Carried orientations

The canonical "smallest view" of a board needs all 8 rotations and flips. Recomputing them from scratch at every step is expensive. Instead the solver keeps all 8 views and updates them as it descends — one quick mask operation per move per view.

The 8 dihedral images of available are carried down the DFS and updated incrementally: placing a queen is one and_not against the move's attack mask in each orientation's frame (child[t] = parent[t] & !a[t]). The key is the lex-min of the 8. Measured ~62 cycles/canon vs ~574 for the from-scratch scatter — a ~9× cheaper key.

Eviction-proof small tables

Any position down to 8-or-fewer free squares is decided by a single lookup into a small table computed once at startup. Those answers can never be forgotten, so those whole sub-games are never searched twice — no matter how many different paths reach them.

The ≤7 region lives in a complete labelled table (one direct byte load, no fingerprint), and the W8 solver adds a complete 32 MiB table for all 8-vertex Node-Kayles games. Both are eviction-free, so a small subgame is always a hit — never re-expanded out of the evicting flat TT. The W8 table alone cut node count ~28% (5.1 B vs 7.1 B).

In-band compact graph

Once a position shrinks to a handful of squares, the solver stops touching the big 256-bit board entirely. It carries a tiny description that fits within one cache line and solves the whole little region inside a scratch memo that stays in the fastest cache—never reaching back out to main memory.

At popcount ≤7 a TinyGraph ([u8;8]×2, fitting within one cache line) is built once; descendants operate on a u8 alive-mask (alive & !closed[i]) instead of four-word board ops. The whole subtree is solved in a thread-private 128-byte stack memo indexed by the alive mask—pure L1, no flat-TT probe, no DRAM, no cross-core coherence.

Serial early-out compare

Finding the "smallest" of the 8 board views means comparing them. The solver compares word by word and stops at the first word that differs — which is almost always the very first one, so most comparisons end immediately. A fancier branch-free version was actually slower.

lex_min8 folds the 8 images with a plain if cand < best; the Bits comparison is lexicographic over four u64 limbs and bails on the first differing limb. Most image pairs differ in word 0, so the compare exits after one limb — and this beat both a branchless lex_lt and a tree reduction in measurement.

Uninitialized scratch buffers

The move list is compacted into a small stack buffer. Zeroing a full board-width array first would cost more than the work it's helping — so the buffer is left uninitialized and only the entries actually written are ever read back.

The move-compaction target is a [MaybeUninit<u8>; MAXV] on the stack — no n²-wide zero-init (which would cost more than the scan it serves). The filter writes exactly the surviving prefix and returns that as &[u8]; MaybeUninit<u8> is layout-identical to u8, so reading back only the written prefix is sound.

The formal version — the 2⁻⁵⁵ fingerprint bound and the multiply-shift map

Fingerprint collisions. A wrong hit requires a foreign key \(k'\neq k\) to (i) route to the same slot and (ii) match the stored 55-bit fingerprint. Conditioned on a colliding probe, the fingerprint — an independent hash half — matches with probability \(p = 2^{-55}\). Over \(N\) colliding probes the expected wrong hits are \(N\cdot 2^{-55}\); at \(N\sim10^{11}\) (Jenrich scale) that is \(\approx 2.8\times10^{-6}\). A fingerprint mismatch is always a correct miss, so only a spurious match can mislead — and the global verdict is cross-checked against the known result.

Multiply-shift (Lemire). To map a uniform 64-bit hash \(h\) into \([0, \ell)\) without a modulo, take

\( \texttt{index}(h) \;=\; \Big\lfloor \dfrac{h \cdot \ell}{2^{64}} \Big\rfloor \;=\; \texttt{((h as u128) * (len as u128)) >> 64}. \)

Reading \(h\) as a fraction \(h/2^{64}\in[0,1)\), this is \(\lfloor \ell\cdot(h/2^{64})\rfloor\) — the slot you'd get by scaling a uniform \([0,1)\) value into \([0,\ell)\). It is near-uniform for any \(\ell\) (the only deviation is the \(2^{-64}\)-scale rounding), so the table is freed from the power-of-two-size constraint that h & (len-1) imposes, at the cost of one full-width multiply instead of an and.

Validation gates (how we know the answer is right)

Every change must pass two checks before it counts. First, the smart solver must agree with the dumb-but-certain solver on all small boards. Second, it must reproduce the exact count of genuinely-different boards at 12×12 — exactly 1,060,823. If a change lost some merges, that count would change; if the table were too small, re-expansion would jump. The 16×16 verdict itself is cross-checked against an independent 2014 computation (Jenrich), which also found the second player wins. The 18×18 verdict, having no prior result to check against, was instead required to survive two independently configured searches agreeing on the winner, the opening move, and the full winning line; and the small-graph evaluator at the solver's core was additionally proved correct by machine (Section 14).

A change is not done until its gate passes: solver_lineage_agrees (every solver matches memo-less naive on small boards), a fresh solve 12 iso-flat --distinct (second-player win, exact distinct 1,060,823, ~1.25× expansion), and the relevant kernel/variant agreement test. Full-scale changes also return a fresh n=16 SECOND. A distinct-count change means lost transposition merges; a re-expansion jump exposes an undersized table or speculative schedule. The verdict cross-checks Jenrich (arXiv:1312.5135) and OEIS A344227. The dense layers are differential-tested against a scalar reference per code (W9–W16 vs wins_rec; W17–W20 vs the wide reference winsw_scalar—the leaves the n=18 verdict bottoms out on). For n=18 the absent external verdict is replaced by two-config cross-validation (W17 vs W18–20, agreeing on verdict + move + 15-ply PV) plus an independent raw-mask oracle on ~3,400 high-square subpositions; and the getK recurrence semantics are machine-checked in Lean 4 (no sorry, standard axioms).

13How the speed was actually won: methodology and war-stories

The algorithms explain what became faster. The method explains why the team kept finding the right algorithms: measure the intended effect separately from its implementation overhead, control the machine, and do not infer a fundamental limit from the current program.

The preceding sections describe the levers; this one describes the keep/kill discipline. Machine state and parallel cutoff timing make single wall measurements noisy, so each experiment needs an interleaved end-to-end result plus diagnostic counters that explain the mechanism. That distinction prevents both false wins and premature rejection of expensive first implementations.

The measurement toolkit

The telemetry table already lists the instruments. The decision process was simpler:

  • Bound the prize first. Counters estimate whether memory waits, branch misses, or instruction delivery are large enough for an idea to matter.
  • Alternate control and variant. Interleaving A/B runs prevents heat and machine state from deciding the comparison.
  • Use wall time to decide and counters to explain. Nodes, cycles per node, and miss rates reveal whether the intended mechanism worked even when the complete version is still slower.

The experiment loop has three gates:

  • Budget: use TMA and event counts to bound the candidate mechanism before implementing it. A branch lever cannot recover memory-stall cycles, and a TLB change cannot exceed the measured page-walk budget.
  • Compare: alternate A/B runs and record wall, total cycles, nodes, cycles/node, CPI, and the event tied to the hypothesis. Wall time is the final objective; normalized counters explain changes in work and cost per unit of work.
  • Classify: close a branch when its causal quantity does not improve. If that quantity moves substantially but incidental overhead dominates, treat the result as an implementation negative and isolate the overhead.
Decision metric versus diagnostic metric

The stopwatch decides whether the solver improved. Work-per-position and miss rates explain why. Neither is sufficient alone: a faster inner loop can search more positions, while a large node cut can still cost too much to discover.

Interleaved wall time is the keep/kill metric. Nodes and cycles/node decompose total cost. ETC supplies the complete example: its first form cut nodes 17.4% but added 27% cycles/node and 9.2% wall; fusion removed redundant work and made it a net win; dynamic ordering then made ETC still more valuable by putting likely cut-trigger children first.

Separate the mechanism from its first implementation

Several useful mechanisms were slower in their first form. ETC began 9.2% slower despite a large node cut, then crossed one minute after fusion and better ordering. Bit extraction had also been dismissed using evidence from tiny graphs. At 12–16 vertices, the arithmetic was different. Testing that scale unlocked four exact layers.

The project distinguishes a mechanism negative, an implementation negative, and a mis-scoped measurement. ABDADA and slot-order frontiers failed their causal economics. ETC began −17.4% nodes / +9.2% wall before fusion made it a win. The earlier pext verdict was based on k≤4 reasoning plus a scalar rectangular reshape; actual K≥12 adj_row_pext was never measured. At the production scale it cut cycles/node 3.8% and moved the WK crossover from 12 to 16, compounding into −35% wall.

Derive the floor; do not inherit it

The programmer used Elon-style first-principles estimates: how much work must be done, and how quickly can the machine plausibly do it? When that estimate lies far below the stopwatch, the difference is an optimization budget. A slow current implementation is not a physical limit.

A defensible performance floor starts with irreducible state resolutions, minimum bytes moved, required arithmetic, and attainable hardware throughput. It does not come from the current call graph or latest failed variant. Dynamic ordering crossed the old 60-second target; scale-correct pext plus W16, then W17 and skip18, reached about 23.4 seconds of search. The working set collapsed from a tight 17 GB regime to a few GB of live contents. Each earlier “floor” described one implementation regime, not the problem. The current estimate is grounded differently—in a measured saturation audit rather than a stopwatch: the search now maxes out no single CPU resource (IPC ≈ 1.4, spare load-queue depth), and its innermost cost is an L1-resident pext dependency chain that no algorithm or AMD Zen5 opcode is known to shorten. That is presented as evidence of proximity to a hardware-and-algorithm limit, not as a claimed wall; the open levers are micro-refactors, one untested pre-pruning idea, and new mathematics.

A parallel case study: interactive transcript search, from hundreds of milliseconds to <100 µs

Before this solver, the same human–agent team built live search over a large archive of coding-session transcripts. The metric was the delay from a keystroke to the first visible results. With caches and prediction disabled, p99 (the delay that 99 of 100 searches beat) fell from hundreds of milliseconds to below 100 microseconds.

That gain came from many changes. In one round, a more selective filter rejected 2.6× more candidates but made the complete program slower. The filter itself was saving time; its rarely used metadata had disrupted the CPU-cache layout of frequently read data. Moving that metadata aside cut median latency by 50% and p95 by 83%.

The working pattern was the same as in the queens solver. Agents implemented and instrumented ideas quickly, but often treated a slow first version as the limit. Human persistence kept mechanisms alive when their internal metrics were promising. First-principles estimates set a target below the roadblocks in the current code. The agent accelerated the engineering loop but did not replace that judgment.

The comparison is methodological, not algorithmic. The cache-disabled benchmark forced a real index lookup on every keystroke and measured time to first result bytes. Reaching p99 <100 µs required compact indexes, early termination, off-critical-path publication, allocation-free buffers, hot/cold data separation, and careful worker placement.

The case-sensitive prefilter improved candidate count 2.6× yet regressed end to end because cold metadata disturbed the hot layout; moving it to a sidecar produced p50 −50% and p95 −83%. Queens ETC repeated that pattern and has now paid off. The later frontier failure showed the complementary judgment: persist when the causal quantity moves, but close the branch when the end-to-end mechanism itself destroys cutoff order.

War-stories

Five episodes show the working loop: symptom, plausible explanation, measured cause, fix, and a rule retained for the next round.

Each card: symptom → the obvious-but-wrong reading → the real cause → the fix → the lesson. Chosen as the most instructive of the lot.

① The phantom speed limit that was really a clogged machine

Symptom. A long, careful optimization pass ended with a firm conclusion: about 36 million positions per second was the ceiling, 50 million was impossible on this hardware, and a 16×16 solve near 3m41s was the wall.

The obvious reading. The reasoning inside that pass was sound on its own terms — the time really was going to random memory waits, and squeezing the math harder genuinely wouldn't raise the rate. So the wall looked real.

The real cause. Three measurement faults stacked up. The machine's memory was already clogged — compressed-RAM swap full of stale pages, a disk cache eating ~13 GB, and around 11 GB of leftover profiling files sitting in RAM-backed scratch space — so the big table never fit and every "solve" ran crippled. A promising dense lookup table had been dismissed on one of these crippled runs. And nobody had forced the memory system onto large pages, so only about 73% of the table got the fast treatment.

The fix. Clean the machine until the table fit, add the dense lookup table, and force every page large. The same solver finished in 2m44s — comfortably under the goal that had just been declared unreachable.

The lesson. Don't trust a "floor" until you've ruled out your own measurement. The estimated best-possible time for this approach is actually under a minute, so 2m44s isn't close to any real limit.

Symptom. A "~36 M/s floor, sub-50 unreachable, n=16 ~3m41s wall" was recorded after an extensive, genuinely competent pass (affinity sweeps, TMA, MLP windowing, a small resident band table) that did bank a real +15% raw M/s and +10.6% n=14 completion — then concluded the wall was hard.

The obvious reading. Self-consistent on its terms: TMA said backend-by-memory ~34%, the high-popcount region is an unbounded random-DRAM probe stream, and node-reduction cuts completion but not the rate (M/s = nodes ÷ wall). It even proved the metric-trap directly — a 1 MB fingerprinted hash hit 63.5 M/s but solved slower (9.30 s vs 8.82 s).

The real cause: three stacked confounds. (1) A degraded box — zram swap full of cold pages from prior OOM storms, ZFS ARC eating ~13 GB, ~11 GB of stale perf.data pinned in tmpfs (= RAM) — so the 17 GB table was capped to 12–13 GB and never reached the eviction-free regime. (2) The complete dense W8 table was dismissed as a wash, but on the capped box and through a slow code path. (3) Huge pages were never forced — MADV_HUGEPAGE is advisory, and the fragmented box only promoted ~57–73% of the multi-GB table to 2 MB pages.

The fix. Clean the box (swap off, ARC capped, tmpfs cleared → ~24 GB free → the 17 GB table fit); rebuild the W8 hook as a one-pass lookup; add MADV_COLLAPSE + prefault for 100%-verified 2 MB coverage. Result: 3m41s → 2m44s. W8 alone won ~28% fewer nodes; collapse added a clean ~5% TLB win at near-identical node count.

The lesson. The instruction-counted floor for this strategy was ~45–60 s, far below the claimed 3m41s wall. That gap demanded an environmental and architectural audit; it did not prove that every proposed optimization would work.

② The counter that cost 2× by bouncing between chiplets

Symptom. Two versions ran the identical core computation, yet one was twice as slow.

The obvious reading. "The machine regressed" — easy to blame the environment when the code looks the same.

The real cause. The slow version bumped a single shared "positions searched" counter on every position. This processor is built from two separate clusters of cores with their own local caches; a shared tally forces that one number to ricochet back and forth between the clusters across the chip's internal fabric, and every core ends up waiting on it.

The fix. Give each core its own private counter and have it report in only about once a second. That alone roughly doubled throughput. The same logic became a blanket rule: nothing shared, no settings lookups, and no memory allocation anywhere in the innermost loop.

The lesson. A "free" bookkeeping line on the hot path isn't free when many cores have to agree on it.

Symptom. Even given the identical kernel of a faster sibling, one solver ran 2× slower at n=14 — purely from the store path.

The obvious reading. "The box regressed" — the kernels matched, so the code looked exonerated.

The real cause. The HX 370 is two CCXs with separate L3s. A single shared atomic incremented every node by all 24 workers bounces over the Infinity Fabric; the per-node fetch_add on the node counter and the per-get HyperLogLog feed were both shared atomics on the hot path.

The fix. Move per-node accounting to a thread-local accumulator, flushed to atomics ~1×/s and drained once after the parallel search. That ~2×'d the solver and lifted every sibling that shared the path. It generalized to a rule: no atomics, no env-var reads, no syscalls (allocation included) in the inner loop — an env-var lock alone serializes every worker, the exact contention a lockless table removes.

The lesson. On a multi-CCX part, a per-node shared write is a cross-fabric coherence stall, not a cheap increment.

③ The profiler pointing at the wrong place entirely

Symptom. Hunting the source of mis-guessed branches, the profiler lit up one spot hard — 76% of the samples in a single helper — and it looked like the answer.

The obvious reading. Rewrite that helper; it's clearly the hotspot.

The real cause. Following the call chain, that helper turned out to be part of a one-time table the program builds at startup. The profiling run had been done on a short 14×14 solve — so short that the startup work dwarfed the actual search. The profiler had faithfully measured the setup, not the game.

The fix. Re-profile the real, long 16×16 run, where startup is a rounding error. The clean picture showed the mis-guesses spread thinly across the search, with one genuinely fixable spot — which became story ④.

The lesson. Profile the workload you actually care about, at full size; a short run measures your setup.

Symptom. Chasing the n=16 branch-miss source, a perf record -e branch-misses on a cheap n=14 run gave a sharp hotspot: 76% of branch-miss samples in a closure shim the compiler hadn't inlined.

The obvious reading. De-closure the hot iteration — the shim is the answer.

The real cause. Annotating with --call-graph dwarf, the caller chain placed the symbol inside a one-time OnceLock table build (a sort) — not the search. n=14 is so short that the startup build dominates the sample.

The fix. Re-profile the n=16 search with the record capped ~100 s post-startup. Clean profile: branch-misses diffuse across the kernel (inherent game-tree branching), with one concretely reducible branch standing out — the move-availability check (story ④).

The lesson. Localize on the production scale: at n=14 a perf record is swamped by the one-time table build that amortizes to nothing at n=16.

④ Deleting a coin-flip branch for a 10% win

Symptom. The counters showed the processor stalling badly — doing well under one useful instruction per cycle.

The obvious reading. "Everything's memory-bound, so there's nothing left to do but wait." Mostly true — but not entirely.

The real cause. At every position the solver walks its parent's move list and keeps the moves still legal. "Still legal?" is close to a coin-flip deep in the tree, and a coin-flip is the worst thing to ask a processor to predict: it guesses, misses about half the time, and pays to unwind the wrong guess. A budget estimate from the counters said these wrong guesses were eating far more time than the memory-directory misses everyone was focused on.

The fix. Remove the question. Write each candidate move down unconditionally and nudge the running count by a 0-or-1 — no "if." Same output, no gamble. Wrong guesses fell about 34%, work-per-step about 9%, for roughly a 10% speedup — beating the much fancier table-layout idea it was competing with.

The lesson. Even when "everything is memory-bound," a coin-flip branch can be worth removing — if the budget says wrong-guesses are large and a controlled test shows the cost wasn't already hidden behind the memory wait.

Symptom. n=16 IPC ≈ 0.79 — the pipeline stalling hard.

The obvious reading. Latency-bound, so bad-spec recovery is presumably hidden behind the TT-probe DRAM stalls and removing it buys nothing.

The real cause. A perf stat budget on a flat-vs-seg n=16 A/B (both ~8.5 T instructions): 143 B branch-misses at ~15–18 cyc ≈ 22–24% of all cycles, vs dTLB misses ~7–8% even at a generous ~75 cyc/page-walk. The one reducible branch (localized after fixing story ③) was the per-square if avail_has8(...) in the move filter — a ~50/50 coin-flip at every node. The hypothesis that the misses were latency-hidden was tested directly by the A/B, not assumed.

The fix. Branchless filter: write the square unconditionally, nc += avail as usize; byte-identical node set. Measured (normalized by node-count-independent CPI): branch-misses 143.5 B → 95.2 B (−33.7%), CPI 1.267 → 1.148 (−9.4%), IPC 0.79 → 0.87, wall ~205 s → 183 s (−11%). The −9.4% CPI is the trustworthy signal and is mechanistically consistent with −48 B misses × ~16 cyc. This single micro-opt (~10%) beat the whole segmented-TT lever (+5%).

The lesson. On a latency-bound search, CPI and branch-miss rate explain the per-node mechanism while the interleaved wall result decides whether it pays. The branch was worth removing because both moved in the predicted direction.

⑤ Writing down what didn't work

Symptom. Plenty of plausible ideas that, when measured, made things worse or did nothing — and the temptation to quietly forget them and possibly try them again later.

The obvious reading. A failed idea is wasted effort; move on.

The real cause / the better view. A measured negative is a result. Several ideas looked clearly good on paper and were genuinely worth trying — a different proof-search algorithm, deeper parallelism, splitting the table across the two core clusters, comparing on raw throughput, an endgame shortcut — yet each lost in a controlled test, sometimes for subtle reasons (splitting the table across clusters does save a real ~13% on coherence, but loses more than that by re-searching positions the two halves no longer share).

The fix. Record each negative with its measurement and its reason, kept on a side branch rather than deleted. The next person reads the ledger and doesn't re-walk the dead end.

The lesson. "All cores at 100% in the monitor" is not the same as useful parallelism; and the cheapest speedup of all is not repeating a failure someone already paid for.

Symptom. A drawer of plausible levers that measured flat or negative, and the risk of someone re-trying them later.

The obvious reading. Discard the failures and move on.

The real cause / better view. A measured negative is a result worth keeping—but only at its actual scope. df-pn explodes past n=8; CCX partitioning loses sharing; a nimber oracle pays too much recursion; slot-order frontiers add 94% nodes. By contrast, the old “pext negative” measured a scalar reshape and reasoned from tiny graphs; it never tested pext row construction at K≥12. Preserving that distinction is what allowed the 33.9-second breakthrough.

The fix. Record each negative with the exact mechanism it invalidates, not a broad label. That prevents repeating the same failed shape without incorrectly banning a materially different use of the underlying idea.

The lesson. "All cores at 100% in htop ≠ useful parallelism" (judge by wall + CPU-seconds); and a documented negative is a banked result.

How the collaboration worked

The coding agents accelerated implementation, instrumentation, and the mechanical search through variants. They did not reliably decide when a regression concealed a useful mechanism; several times they treated the latest bottleneck as a floor.

The overnight brief made those roles concrete. The human set a 30-second target, gave Claude permission to branch, and told it to keep working without replies. Claude re-read the earlier measurements, built alternatives, and returned with a 33.9-second run. The target determined when to continue; the agent made a broad experimental search practical.

The collaboration had distinct roles:

  • Human judgment set the target and the stopping rule. First-principles estimates bounded the plausible floor from state work, bytes moved, and machine throughput. Mechanism metrics determined whether a regression deserved another pass.
  • Coding agents compressed—and sometimes autonomously drove—the experiment cycle. Under the overnight 30-second mission, Claude profiled the real run, separated measured facts from extrapolation, built pext row extraction, swept K13–K16, validated the gates, and banked the winning default. Earlier sessions still repeatedly needed human direction to continue past local negatives or asserted floors.
  • Literature supplied reusable machinery. Node Kayles and Sprague–Grundy anchored correctness; HyperLogLog sized the state set; BuRR, fastrange, graph refinement, and DDD supplied candidate architectures.
  • The written ledger made the loop cumulative. Validation gates preserved correctness, while measured negatives recorded exactly which mechanism failed and under what conditions.

The tools made each iteration faster. Human performance intuition decided which iterations were still worth doing.

The scope is deliberately "2-lite": the bit-level pext code layout and decode are left to the existing differential tests (where they are cheapest to check), and the Lean↔Rust correspondence is hand-audited rather than mechanically transported. The outsized theoretical lever remains an even-n pairing theorem analogous to the odd-n O(1) result. None is known; PSPACE-completeness of generalized Node Kayles does not rule one out for this board family.

Appendix: iso-dense, end to end

This appendix is deliberately self-contained. It describes the current K=17 default as an algorithm, then explains why each layer exists and which earlier mechanism it replaces. The short version is: search positions above 17 available squares with a flat D₄-keyed TT; skip the TT entirely at exactly 18 squares (the one band that is safe to forget); evaluate pc≤17 exactly as small Node-Kayles graphs; order moves by current forcing power; ETC-probe recurse children in that order; parallelize only where the boolean cutoff makes parallel work non-speculative.

A. The state and recurrence

A position is represented by a 256-bit available mask. Placing a queen at square s removes s plus every available square attacked by it:

child = available & !attack[s]

Nothing else about move history matters. Therefore distinct move orders that produce the same available mask are one state—a transposition. The solver needs one boolean recurrence:

\(W(P)=\exists m\in moves(P):\neg W(P_m)\)

An empty position is losing for the mover. A node is winning as soon as one losing child is found; that is the cutoff that makes move order valuable and uncontrolled parallelism dangerous.

B. What is carried down the search

For large positions, iso-dense carries all eight D₄ orientations of the available mask. A child updates those orientations incrementally instead of rotating/reflection-folding a fresh 256-bit mask. The lexicographically smallest orientation is the TT key. It also carries a filtered move list: because availability only shrinks, each child filters its parent's already-short list rather than scanning all 256 squares.

This is one place where the name can mislead. The current champion does not run a graph-isomorphism canonicalizer in its live hot path. That was the key idea in iso-burr and iso-flat. With DK=17, every position with at most seventeen available squares is intercepted by WK first; positions above the dense ceiling use the cheaper D₄ key—except the pc=18 band, which skips the key, probe, and store entirely (next).

C. The flat transposition table for pc ≥ 19, and the pc=18 skip

The live TT is one flat array of 8-byte atomic slots. A 128-bit hash is split into an indexing half (route) and an independent 55-bit fingerprint plus value. A probe is one relaxed atomic load; a put is one relaxed store. Same-key races are benign because the game value is deterministic, while a foreign key must collide in both slot and fingerprint to look like a hit.

The table is prefaulted and collapsed into 2 MiB huge pages. It has no locks, per-worker shards, or BuRR segments. That last point is the decisive difference from the compressed branch:

W16, then W17, have changed its economics: the allocation is far from full, holding only a few GB of live contents. A smaller TT nevertheless measured 9.5% slower from extra eviction, so low occupancy is not permission to shrink it without an A/B.

The pc=18 skip (skip18). One band is treated specially. At pc=18 the solver builds no canonical key, runs no probe, and writes no entry; it simply recurses. This is sound because pc=18 is the only band every one of whose children is a getK leaf (pc≤17): if a pc=18 node is reached again through a different move order, re-expanding it costs exactly one bounded getK sweep, never an unmemoized subtree, so the omission can never cascade. The band is also ~100% cold in practice (a 0.3% entry-probe hit rate), so the put was almost pure waste. Removing the work—canonicalization is the search's single largest branch-mispredict site—cut −2.5% wall / −3.6% cycles, verdict-preserving. The band is unique: every extension ({19}, contiguous ranges, a fractional skip) measured net-negative, because any band above 18 has children that are themselves memoized and would be re-probed cold on re-expansion.

D. W0..W8: a complete, eviction-proof base

At pc≤8 the geometry of the board no longer matters once the conflict graph is built. Label the live squares 0..k−1 and encode the upper triangle of their adjacency matrix. For k=8 that is 8·7/2 = 28 bits, so every labelled graph indexes a bit in W8. Tables W0 through W8 are built bottom-up once and occupy about 32 MiB in total.

W[k][graph] = any vertex v such that
              W[child induced by graph - N[v]] == LOSS

The table is complete, not a cache: there is no fingerprint, collision, eviction, canonicalization, or miss. Graph values are invariant under relabelling, so any deterministic ordering of live squares is valid. This removed the repeated ≤8 subtree expansion that a flat TT could forget.

E. W9..W17: exact tables that are never materialized

A literal W9 bitset would require 2³⁶ bits—8 GiB—and W10 would require 2⁴⁵ bits. Instead, getK computes only the requested row of the conceptual table. For each possible move it removes the chosen vertex and its neighbors, projects the surviving induced graph, and calls the lower exact layer:

getK(code):
    adjacency = extract_rows(code)
    for vertex v in 0..K:
        alive = all_vertices - v - neighbors(v)
        child_code = pext(code, induced_edge_mask[alive])
        if lower_layer(popcount(alive), child_code) == LOSS:
            return WIN
    return LOSS

BMI2 pext is the enabling primitive in two places. adj_row_pext compacts each board attack row into K labelled adjacency bits, avoiding scalar edge tests. Inside getK, an induced-edge mask projects a child code while preserving edge order. K≤11 fits in u64; K≤16 fits in u128; K=17's 136-bit code runs on a three-word path. There is no heap allocation, graph canonicalization, hash, or TT probe below the ceiling. At pc=17 the getK children are additionally swept most-forcing-first, so the evaluator returns WIN at the earliest losing child rather than after a fixed pass.

F. The hot-path dispatch

The record path first orders children, then applies the dense dispatch and ETC:

children = legal_moves()
for child in children:
    child.pc = popcount(child.available)
sort children by child.pc ascending       // most forcing; pc==0 first

ETC-probe children with pc > DK in that order
if a cached child is LOSS: return WIN

for child in children:
    if child.pc == 0:       return WIN
    else if child.pc <= DK: lost = !getK(child.pc, graph_code(child))  // DK=17
    else if child.pc == 18: lost = !wins_inc_skip(child)  // recurse, no key/probe/put
    else:                   lost = !wins_inc(child)        // flat TT + DFS
    if lost: return WIN
return LOSS

DK, the order/ETC mode, and the skip18 flag are const generics selected once at the root/subtree handoff; the default DK is 17. The pc=18 branch recurses without building a canonical key, probing, or storing—sound because every pc=18 child is a getK leaf, so a re-expansion costs one bounded sweep rather than an unmemoized subtree. The earlier standalone memo-less get17 was a separate validated mode, reverted after its A/B loss; resolving pc=17 inside the W hierarchy is what ships. Off modes remain production-byte-identical, which makes the comparisons causal.

G. Parallelism: where it is sound and where it stopped paying

Symmetry-distinct opening roots are distributed across Rayon workers sharing the TT. Within a root, only prove-loss levels—where every child must be refuted—fan out without speculation. Prove-win levels remain ordered and sequential so the first losing child can cut off its siblings.

This creates a tail dominated by one opening root, but attempts to fill idle cores by splitting it changed the work: finer splitting, ABDADA deferral, warm restart, and frontier work stealing increased or merely rearranged concurrent re-expansion. The best tuned work stealing was +8.7% nodes and +13.3% wall. iso-dense therefore accepts imperfect utilization when preserving transpositions and cutoff order is cheaper. Its default warm restart is retained for a small node trim but is wall-neutral.

H. How the major levers compounded

After W12, 87–88% of remaining probe cost sat at pc=13..21, and sorted probe streams measured roughly 5× faster. Fused M_WAVE banked the safe part—ETC batch probes without changing recursive child order—and reached 1m32s. The proposed producer/consumer frontier required slot-order descent, however. Its cheap de-risk measured +94% n=16 nodes. A move-order-preserving L0 dedup cache also lost (+5.2% total cycles). The locality was real, but its required search order destroyed almost twice as many cutoffs.

M_ORD used that measurement constructively: replace static empty-board degree with the child's current remaining-popcount, ascending. It cut wall 30.2%. M_ORD_W applied ETC to those ranked recurse children and crossed one minute; a top-k ETC budget was later measured negative because cut triggers were broadly distributed.

The next profile put graph-code build plus getK evaluation near 29% of cycles. Earlier work had rejected a scalar reshape and reasoned against pext from tiny k≤4 data. Re-testing actual K≥12 row extraction gave a direct 3.8% cycles/node win, reduced W13's incremental cost, and made K13–K16 all profitable. The lesson is not “retry every loss”; it is to separate what was measured from what was merely generalized beyond the measured regime.

Lifting the ceiling once more to K=17 (pc=17 as a degree-ordered getK leaf) won −8.6%..−13% wall, and that is where the ceiling sweet spot sits. The final lever inverted the direction: rather than evaluate more, skip18 stores less, removing the canonical key, probe, and put for the cascade-free pc=18 band (−2.5% wall). At that point the levers ran out. Move ordering was retired (its refinement menu—reply-degree, summed forcing power, countermove/killer, global history—measured dead in live A/B); a 2nd-ply refutation lever has a −13% oracle ceiling but no cheap predictor for the rare refuting reply; a treewidth/separator DP, value-bucketing, and a family of graph reductions all measured NO-GO against exact nimbers. A cycle-accurate saturation audit then found the search bound by no single resource—the innermost cost is an L1-resident pext dependency chain with no known shortening—so the project's framing turned from “the next speedup” to “present the evidence of proximity to a hardware-and-algorithm limit, and let the open mathematics decide.”

I. Correctness and trust boundary

  • Every dense evaluator is compared with a scalar recurrence over thousands of graph codes.
  • All solver variants agree with memo-less naive on small boards.
  • The iso-flat control reproduces the exact n=12 distinct count, 1,060,823.
  • Behavior-changing full-scale experiments must return n=16 SECOND.
  • Dynamic-order and order-plus-ETC variants match the validated default through the recorded n=16 verdict.
  • The final n=16 verdict is independently cross-checked against Jenrich's 2014 71.46-billion-call computation.
  • The n=18 first-player verdict is corroborated by two independently configured exhaustive searches—different dense leaf evaluators (W17 vs W18–20)—agreeing on the verdict, the winning move I9, and the 15-ply principal variation at node counts differing by more than 2×.
  • An independent raw-mask negamax oracle (no getK, no canonicalization, no table) checks both solvers on ~3,400 18×18 subpositions spanning the high-index squares—the path that caught the u8-truncation bug at pc=3.
  • The getK leaf evaluator's recurrence semantics are machine-checked in Lean 4 (the win/loss recurrence and its termination, isomorphism and induced-subgraph invariances, the build recurrence, and the Grundy characterization); no sorry, standard axioms only.

The 55-bit TT fingerprint makes a false hit probabilistic, but the independent verdict and solver/kernel cross-checks make that risk observable at multiple levels. For n=18, where no prior verdict existed, that external safety net is replaced by two-way cross-validation between independently configured searches, the independent-oracle subposition differential, and a machine-checked leaf evaluator. The residual trusted component is the generic high-popcount α-β combination logic over the I9 subtree, validated at n≤16 and exercised identically by both configs.

Appendix: detailed project timeline

This is the research log behind the compact timeline in the main report. Times and commit identifiers come from the repository; performance figures come from recorded controlled runs. “Negative” means an implementation was built and measured, not merely rejected in discussion.

Sunday, June 14 — the game arrives as an adversarial benchmark

Starting point. The repository's main work was an Othello engine. A university mathematics professor who coaches the programmer's sixth-grade son in university-level math had pointed him at the Non-Attacking Queens Game, with minimax/alpha–beta to learn. Helping with it became its own performance project. At 23:04, commit 1b58a88 added a small exact-search binary: represent attacked squares, recurse over legal placements, and return whether the mover can force the last move.

Unknowns. There was no memory strategy, solver lineage, or n=16 plan. The useful property was that the rules are simple while the game graph has enormous transposition density—ideal for testing canonicalization, memoization, and parallel cutoffs without evaluation-function ambiguity.

Monday, June 15 — map the state space and build the classical solver ladder

Progress. In the morning, a fixed-size TT and root parallelism landed (5bd31fb), followed by the centre-plus-180° mirror theorem for every odd board (530eae2), an explicit solver lineage (1918906), and an OEIS-validated Sprague–Grundy solver (86275ad). The D₄ parallel solver produced the project's first n=16 finish in about 56 minutes. Canonicalizing the sparse available mask rather than the dense blocked mask made the same D₄ operation about 2.4× cheaper.

Telemetry. HyperLogLog plus exact-small-n distinct counting (b603e15 and follow-ups) separated node visits from actual states. Re-expansion reporting and TT cardinality made the n=16 wall quantitative instead of rhetorical. A compact fingerprint slot (6c32617) shrank storage from roughly 40 bytes to 8 bytes per slot; an unsharded lockless TT, prefetch, huge pages, and arbitrary-size fastrange indexing filled the available RAM efficiently.

Graph equivalence. count --iso and value-consistency tests (70d19f5, 594ab4e) showed that canonicalizing the available-square conflict graph safely merged about 3.4× more states than board D₄. This made n=16 capacity plausible.

False starts and roadblocks. df-pn was correct but already showed the proof-number/transposition pathology. Effective-degree and history-style move ordering did not move the wall. Depth-preferred TT replacement and naive deeper parallelism created more re-expansion. A free-involution losing-position certificate fired too rarely. Checkpoint/dump/reload improved operability but could not make an undersized flat table remember billions of states.

End-of-day model. The problem was no longer “make recursion faster.” It was “fit a transposition-dense DAG without making every key or lookup too expensive.”

Tuesday, June 16 — solve capacity with BuRR, then discover the lookup tax

Compressed storage track. A BuRR static-retrieval archive landed at 08:57 (d0c1899). It could freeze known keys into roughly 1.1 bits/value-key and verify them, providing the first credible route around flat-TT eviction. Root-set and branching telemetry measured cross-root reuse and the proof-DAG gap; theoretical-floor and n=18 notes sized future storage.

Kernel progress. The incremental solver (1b8bdcd) carried orientations and attacked state down the tree, improving n=14 by about 1.5× at the same node set. Checkpoint UX and profile-guided optimization (PGO) were aligned with that path.

Live BuRR branch. burr made BuRR the primary live LSM store (8edc10a), with prefetched Bloom filters and shared hashing. The first design froze monolithically and OOMed; append-only segments, per-segment Blooms, and a hard memory cap (64d86db) made it survive full scale.

First compressed n=16 finish. Combining graph-isomorphic keys with the eviction-free archive produced iso-burr: SECOND in 29m23s. Unlike the earlier ~56-minute D₄ finish, this run kept re-expansion near 1.15× and proved the full state set could fit. Jenrich's verdict independently matched.

Roadblock exposed. The graph key itself was initially ~2.2× underwater, despite its 3.4× merge. More importantly, live BuRR misses walked a growing cascade of immutable segments, so throughput fell as the search progressed. The first complete solution therefore identified the next bottleneck: lookup shape, not storage volume.

Wednesday, June 17 — drop the compressed live store; make every lookup O(1)

Combination that lost. fused tried to combine incremental state, graph-isomorphic keys, and live BuRR freezing. It finished n=16 in 38m26s—slower than iso-burr—because redundant keying and the segment cascade outweighed the fusion.

Measurement confound. Several n=14 regressions were initially blamed on code, then traced to machine state and shared counters. The TT huge-page advice was also silently failing with EINVAL on a misaligned interior pointer until b3bc58f. Thread-local hot counters and explicit CPU affinity removed coherence and scheduling noise. This episode produced the pre-flight and interleaved-A/B discipline used afterward.

Architectural reset. iso-flat (241000f) kept the useful selective key but dropped BuRR and the LSM cascade for one flat lockless TT. Initially it fit but ran near 1 M node/s because live Weisfeiler–Leman canonicalization was too expensive. Restricting graph-iso to the tiny, deep band and recovering the fused kernel raised sustained throughput above 12 M/s (9331f9b).

Rapid milestones. n=16 fell to 9m11s, then 8m20s with KEY_MAX=7, then 7m02s with TT sizing. Hash carry, tighter keys, branch/work reductions, and a serial hot-path rewrite brought the full result to 4m11s. An incremental move-list change cut instructions 25% yet lost 5.7% wall—an early warning that instruction count alone does not predict a latency-bound solve.

End-of-day model. Flat lookup was the right live store. The remaining waste was deep small positions being evicted from a multi-gigabyte cache and recomputed.

Thursday, June 18 — make the deep tail complete and overturn a false floor

Tail extraction. pc≤7 became a compact local graph game carried without the 256-bit board, first in L1 memo and then in a complete direct labelled table. The best version deliberately avoided canonicalization: direct labelled indexing was ~15% faster even though it merged less, because the complete table never misses.

Measured negatives. Per-node MLP windows increased instructions enough to erase latency overlap; an L2 fingerprinted hash was worse than the direct 2 MiB table; CCX partitioning saved a real coherence cost but lost more through cross-CCX re-search. These results narrowed the viable tail design to complete shared values.

W8 result. iso-window precomputed every labelled Node-Kayles value through 8 vertices and terminated the search at pc=8. On a clean box with forced 2 MiB collapse, n=16 reached 2m44s (2457646). This overturned a recently declared ~3m41 “floor,” which had been measured with full zram, a 13 GB ARC, stale tmpfs profiles, a capped TT, and incomplete huge-page promotion.

Instrumentation and kernel work. Per-pc write histograms and a segmented TT were added; a branchless availability filter cut branch misses ~34% and cycles about 10%. Together segmentation and the filter gained roughly 16% throughput. Set-associative bands were only break-even.

Large theoretical win that lost. A component-nimber oracle cut nodes up to 74% but made wall time 6.6× worse because mex requires all children and WL/nimber recursion dominated. Dense-nimber coverage telemetry then killed the proposed salvage. The lesson became explicit: a node saved is worth only the cost of the node it replaces.

Friday, June 19 — locate the hot pc bands and replace them with WK

Fresh attribution. Top-down measurements found co-dominant memory and frontend/i-cache cost; return misprediction was effectively zero, so recursion removal itself had no prize. Collapsing a duplicated PROVE_LOSS monomorph cut i-cache MPKI 74%.

Dense-block negative and new attribution. A generic dense-block solver was net-negative because local boundary work lost transposition sharing. The new QUEENS_PROF stratified timer then corrected the initial interpretation: 63.5% of random-probe cost was at pc 9–12, not the upper tree. The block failed on per-resolution compute, not lack of coverage.

WK result. The solver computed one conceptual dense-table row without storing the table or introducing a private boundary. W9 (bcb70e6) used BMI2 projection into complete W0..W8 and reached 2m12s. The generalized hierarchy reached W11 / 1m44s (7f7bbf9) and W12 / 1m39s (84800a2). W12 was the first 66-bit two-word evaluator.

Crossover measured, not assumed. W13 (fc3865f) removed another ~15% of n=16 nodes but regressed wall ~4%; K=12 became the default optimum. Building the wide code as two u64 words cut w12_get's own cycles. W12 also reduced TT occupancy because pc≤12 is never stored.

Parallelism branch closes. Root/utilization measurements showed the giant-root tail, but finer split/taper added re-expansion and warm restart was wall-neutral. A recursive-to-explicit-stack conversion initially cost 4.3%, then was optimized to parity; it was retained only as infrastructure for frontier experiments.

Saturday, June 20 — stop scheduling duplicate work; reorder it

Coordination experiments. ABDADA in-flight markers were implemented and validated (773ba8d) but could not profitably defer the large pc 13–21 nodes. Frontier work stealing then published even-depth children to idle cores. After tail gating and extensive tuning, the best variant still added 8.7% nodes and 13.3% wall. This was the fifth failed attempt to extract more DFS-local parallelism.

Re-anchor on the actual champion. Fresh W12 root timing showed one root lasting ~94% of wall and only a 17.5-second solo tail; the scheduling ceiling was therefore small. TMA remained co-dominant memory (~30%) and frontend (~31%). The unfused baseline at this point was about 1m33s / 1.93 B nodes.

Probe economics. pc 13–21 carried ~87% of remaining get cost, each around 176–180 cycles with 34–39% hit rates. Skipping the checks was refuted by about 22× expected value and independently by W13's wall regression.

Locality result. A random-versus-sorted TT microbenchmark found that sorting by slot changes the hardware regime: one-core sorted depth-32 reached 274.7 M probes/s versus 48.6 M/s random depth-1; four sorted consumers reached 763.8 M/s and the subsystem saturated near 780 M/s. The advantage survived contention and was largest with few consumers—the exact shape needed to use idle cores for sorting rather than duplicate searching.

ETC turns positive. The first in-DFS ETC implementation (e80cfc9) cut nodes 17.4% but added 9.2% wall. Fusing gather with descent removed rebuilt keys and double probes; M_WAVE became the 1m32s / 1.70 B default.

Approach B loses as built. Cold sizing found abundant same-row locality and an apparent dedup ceiling, but slot-order descent added 94% nodes at n=16; an order-preserving L0 dedup cache added 5.2% total cycles with no real node cut. Those implementations were banked as negatives. The open condition is stricter: deduplicate without disturbing the consumer's move order or paying per-probe overhead.

Dynamic ordering breaks the wall. The +94% result showed that move order was worth roughly 2× the work. Replacing static empty-board degree with current child0.popcount() ordering cut 34.3% of nodes and 30.2% wall versus M_WAVE; the clean run reached 1m02s / 1,136,583,273 nodes. ETC on top cut another 18.4% of nodes and 6.5% total cycles, producing 59.2s / ~919 M nodes.

End-of-day state. Dynamic order plus ETC crossed one minute and became the default. A top-k ETC budget was then measured negative because cut triggers were distributed rather than concentrated; insertion-sort and hot-loop tuning continued toward ~52 seconds.

Sunday, June 21 — re-test pext at the real scale and extend the exact tail to W16

Read the old negative literally. The earlier getK work had measured a scalar rectangular reshape and reasoned about pext from tiny k≤4 cases. It had never measured one pext-built adjacency row per vertex at K≥12, where 12 extractions replace 66 scalar edge tests.

Small direct win, large compound win. adj_row_pext cut n=16 cycles/node 3.8%. More importantly, it reduced the incremental cost of wider getK layers. W13, formerly a wall regression, became positive; W14, W15, and W16 followed. K16's 120-bit code fits the existing u128 representation.

New record. K16 cut n=14 deterministic nodes 50% versus K12 and n=16 nodes 57%; the cut was identical across 12–16 GB tables, proving it was inherent subtree removal rather than extra eviction. The clean 17 GB run returned SECOND in 33.9s / 396 M nodes, with the TT only 16.5% full.

A crossover that looked terminal, but wasn't. A table-free, standalone memo-less get17 was implemented and validated, but its n=16 A/B was −19.4% nodes, +30.7% cycles/node, +5.7% wall: recomputing each shallow, transposition-heavy pc=17 occurrence in isolation lost to a memoized TT hit. That standalone form was reverted. The next session reopened it from a different angle—pc=17 resolved inside the W hierarchy as a getK leaf, with its children reused exact layers and swept most-forcing-first—and that form won.

Monday, June 22 — push the ceiling to W17, then stop storing the one safe band

W17 as a getK leaf. A 192-bit three-word labelled code lifts the exact ceiling to K=17, resolving pc=17 (~21% of nodes) as a getK leaf with no flat-TT probe, and its child sweep is degree-ordered so the evaluator returns at the earliest losing reply. Against the W16 default this won −8.6%..−13% wall, bringing the search to ~24.5s. K=17 is the wall sweet spot: building W18–W20 cuts another ~52% of nodes but conserves the work (cycles/node rise in proportion), so the wall flattens. The default ceiling moved K=16→17.

skip18. The next gain inverted the direction—store less, not compute more. For pc=18 nodes the solver now builds no canonical key, runs no probe, and writes no entry. This is sound for exactly this band: pc=18 is the only size every one of whose children is a getK leaf (pc≤17), so a re-expanded pc=18 node re-runs one bounded getK sweep and never an unmemoized subtree. The band is ~100% cold anyway (0.3% probe-hit). Skipping the work—canonicalization being the single largest branch-mispredict site—won −2.5% wall / −3.6% cycles, verdict-preserving (n=12 distinct exact at 1,060,823). It is the complete band-skip lever: {19}, contiguous ranges, and a fractional skip all measured net-negative, because any higher band's children are themselves memoized and would be re-probed cold. New clean-box record: ~23.4s search / 307,608,950 nodes / SECOND.

Structural levers ruled out. A treewidth/separator DP looked promising—the deep tail graphs are tree-like (min-fill width ~7–10 at the mass bands, even lower at n=16 than n=14)—but a validated Node-Kayles constant-factor probe killed it: the subtree such a DP would replace is tiny (median 80–768 positions at the mass bands) against a DP cost of \(3^{w+1}\), so not one sampled graph paid. The deep tail is expensive by breadth, not depth, and a per-instance FPT method cannot reduce breadth. The rest of the structural-reduction cluster followed: value-bucketing (1-WL already separates nearly every deep graph, so the only value-pure key is isomorphism itself), leaf/simplicial/dominated-vertex reductions (dominated-vertex deletion is unsound; the one sound novel rule fires on ≤0.4% of graphs), and structural-involution pairing (the deep graphs are structurally rigid, fixed-point-free involutions fire on 0.00%) all measured NO-GO against exact nimbers.

Tuesday, June 23 — measure the new solver's saturation; confirm near-floor

Saturation deep-dive. A per-tick per-worker time-series sampler plus perf stat -I refined the “work-starvation tail” downward: the box stays ~94% utilized for the whole run (mean ~23/24 cores producing nodes), and deep starvation (<2 cores) is only ~0.3s in a few events at the very end. The tail is slower per node, not idle—higher-pc getK sweeps cost more. A Zen5 dispatch-stall audit found the search saturates no single resource: IPC ≈ 1.4 against a peak near 6, load-queue-full ~0.5–0.7%, store-queue-full ~1%. The load queue's large headroom is unused MLP the serial DFS probe cannot fill—which mechanistically explains why every prefetch lever died. The low IPC is a distributed frontend (i-cache, getK code) + backend stall, not one wall.

The 2nd-ply refutation lever. The one sizeable node-count prize left: front-load the refuting reply on the wall-determining root. The oracle is real—front-loading the known refutation cut that root −72% and the full run −13%/−10%—but no cheap signal ranks a cheap refutation early. Degree pushes it later, cross-root overlap is uniform, subtree-size is similar, and a symmetry-defect heuristic turned out to be an anti-signal (the most-symmetric replies are expensive non-refutations). Parallelizing the 2nd ply re-expands (+393% nodes). One untested idea remains: a residual-stabilizer orbit-dedup of the 2nd-ply move set on symmetry-axis openings—sound pre-pruning, not a memo-skip, so no cascade.

Four-front sweep → near-floor, one micro-win. Read-only agents swept the getK/W17 math, the refutation/symmetry math, a re-audit of banked lessons, and the Zen5 instruction set. The convergent finding: the dominant cost is an irreducible L1-resident pext dependency chain that no algorithm or AMD Zen5 opcode is known to shorten—the full-width AVX-512 datapath does not revive the dead getK SIMD, and pext has no vector equivalent. The audit also caught a banked-lesson regression: get17 did a per-node OnceLock+Box dereference (the same pointer-chase the W8 flat arena had fixed). Resolving the W17 induced table into a contiguous &'static field landed −0.55% (balanced ABBA-paired A/B; a naive always-A-first A/B had read −3.4% as a thermal artifact, a banked method lesson). The standing framing: n=16 is comfortably solved in well under 30 seconds end to end; what remains is micro-refactors, the one untested pre-pruning idea, and multi-session research—a canonical-component value layer, and an even-n pairing theorem that would beat silicon outright.

What changed over nine days

The solver did not simply collect features. BuRR proved that compression could fit the state set, then disappeared from the live search. Graph hashing established a useful equivalence, then WK bypassed it. Sorted frontiers lost, but their 94% node increase exposed the value of move order. W13 lost because graph construction was expensive; pext reduced that cost enough to make W13–W17 pay. A standalone memo-less get17 lost outright, then won when reframed as a getK leaf inside the hierarchy. And the last gain ran opposite to all the others—skip18 made the solver store less, removing the bookkeeping for the one band it is provably safe to forget.

ETC was 9.2% slower before fusion and ordering turned it into a win. Pext had been dismissed using evidence from the wrong scale before it helped reach 33.9 seconds, then W17 and skip18 carried the search to ~23.4 seconds. The final two days then did the harder thing: they measured the solver carefully enough to conclude that the obvious gains were gone—move ordering exhausted, structural reductions ruled out against exact nimbers, the throughput core bound by an irreducible instruction-level dependency chain—and that the open frontier had moved from cycles to mathematics. Coding agents shortened the build-and-measure cycle and, under the overnight mission, autonomously drove much of it; but they repeatedly treated prototypes and current bottlenecks as floors. Human persistence and first-principles estimates determined which negative results deserved another implementation, and when a measured limit had finally earned its evidence. The agents did not reliably make that call on their own.
